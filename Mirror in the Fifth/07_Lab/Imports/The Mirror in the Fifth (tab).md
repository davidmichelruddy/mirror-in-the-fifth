# The Mirror in the Fifth

Oct 23, 2026 · @David Michel-Ruddy

*A Polar Theory of Harmony*

## Thesis

**Harmonic energy is the listener's search for the anchor.**

Harmony is the relationship between sounds, not the purity of a single tone. Its complexity begins the moment we step away from the unison (1:1). The octave (2:1) returns the same pitch; the perfect fifth (3:2) is the first overtone relationship that produces something new from the fundamental. That generative relationship is what we experience as harmony, so the unit of harmony is the fifth, not the single pitch: a polar unit, with two poles and the energy that runs between them.

Each step up in numerical complexity opens a new dimension: 2 gives identity (the octave), 3 gives generation (the spine of fifths), 5 gives color (the thirds).

Treat the C–G dyad as one object with two poles, and major and minor become mirror images: one act of growth, begun from opposite ends.

**Two colors of one home.** We hear major and minor as two colors of one home. Most symmetric theories flip harmony around a single tone, which turns C major into F minor and then has to explain why our ears disagree. This book flips around the fifth instead, so the mirror and the experience coincide: C minor is C major upside down, and nothing needs renaming. It is the only flip that keeps the unit whole, and its center sits half a fifth from home, which is why color mirrors while the root stays put.

**The heart: ancestor and offspring.** Every pitch implies its origin. A fifth is the 2nd and 3rd harmonics of a fundamental, so hearing C–G implies the C beneath it; the ear supplies the source the way it supplies a missing fundamental. Along the spine, each note is the offspring of the note a fifth below and the ancestor of the note a fifth above. Upward is generation; downward is return to the source. That is why the root of any fifth is its bottom note, and why gravity points one way while color mirrors. It asks for one abstraction, hearing a pitch as carrying its lineage, and it is the heart of the framework.

**The ear fills in the source.** This is a well-documented effect, the missing fundamental (also called residue or virtual pitch): when a sound contains harmonics of a fundamental, listeners hear that fundamental even if it is absent, as with a bass line on a small speaker that cannot reproduce it. The effect is strongest when the tones line up as low, consecutive members of one overtone series. A fifth is harmonics 2 and 3, so it points cleanly to the note an octave below its bottom. A major triad is harmonics 4, 5 and 6 of one fundamental, so it implies its source strongly. A minor triad (10:12:15) fits no simple low series, so its implied fundamental is weak, and the ear leans on the fifth inside it, which still points to C. Gravity, in other words, is the ear reaching for the source.

**The search for the anchor.** Harmonic motion is felt as the ear locating home, and every energy in the framework reads through that search:

- **Rest** is the anchor found: a major tonic implies its source most clearly of any chord, and a minor tonic implies it through its fifth.
- **Tension** is the anchor in doubt. Anchorless shapes imply no source of their own; the diminished seventh points to four possible homes, the augmented triad to three.
- **Resolution** is the anchor confirmed: B lands on C, F lands on E, and the source is clear again.
- **Modulation** is the anchor relocated; the express lanes measure how far the ear must travel to find the new one.
- **Color** is how a relationship feels relative to the anchor once the ear knows where home is.

Key-finding research (Krumhansl's tonal hierarchies) shows listeners continuously inferring a tonal center, and Huron's *Sweet Anticipation* treats musical tension as expectation and its resolution. This framework gives that search a map.

**Two traditions, one unit.** Mathieu, drawing on Indian classical practice, shows that all relationships stem from the generative overtone source, and that the first act of generation, the fifth, creates a pole: ancestor and offspring, dark and light. Levy centers his mirror on a single tone, the generator, with its poles the fifths on either side, and explains the heard root with a later correction, telluric adaptation (from Coleman's summary; verify in Levy, and see Beside Levy and Coleman). This frame joins the two. It designates the fifth as the unit, places the polarity inside it, and shows gravity spatially, so the root at the bottom of any fifth is a property of the unit, not an adaptation.

The symmetry itself is known and heard every day; what this book offers is a clearer mental model. Other conceptions need a caveat or a mental leap to explain minor, the modes or the root. With the polar unit as the anchor, fewer leaps are needed (gravity is itself one added principle), and the real question comes into view: how the energies of these relationships work.

Musicians already use major and minor this way: parallel switches, borrowed iv, the Picardy third. Pitch-centric theory still explains minor as major with a lowered third, an offset rather than a reflection. This book closes that gap between practice and explanation.

The argument builds in five steps, each adding one idea: a line of fifths, a plane of color, a slope of gravity, a circle that cannot close, and the helix that results.

## The founding premise: generation before ancestry

*Premise.* Generation comes first. Pitches are related because one sounds the other as an overtone; that is a fact about the sound. Ancestry is the ear running that relation backward, inferring a source it does not hear. So every offspring claim in the book is about the sound, and every ancestor claim is about listening.

- **The physics runs one way.** A tone carries its harmonics above it; it does not carry the tones that would have produced it. *Supported:* the harmonic series.
- **Ancestry is inference.** Given harmonics, the ear supplies a fundamental that isn't sounding (the missing fundamental, or residue pitch, after Schouten; see The ear fills in the source). *Supported* for single complex tones. Extending it to chord roots follows Terhardt's virtual-pitch model, developed by Parncutt: *Hypothesis*, adopted.
- **The words follow the premise.** Offspring is what a pitch sounds. Ancestor is what the ear infers sounded it. Any ancestor claim should be restatable as "the ear infers…".

**What the premise settles.**

- **The unit forms by generation.** A root and the fifth it generates make a unit. A root whose fifth is absent has no unit: Locrian. A note missing only its ancestor still forms one: Lydian's F lacks B♭ but still generates C.
- **Minor needs no undertone series.** G is a real overtone of C (3rd partial) and of E♭ (5th partial). "Two ancestors, one offspring" is overtones read backward. This keeps the dualist insight (von Oettingen's phonicity) without the dualist physics.
- **The root is the generator.** Gravity is the ear tracing back toward it.

**What it does not settle on its own.** Why the trace stops at home: F generates C, so ancestry alone would keep falling flatward. The answer is claim 8: gravity follows the clues that are actually sounding, and nothing sounding supports F.

**Why this is a premise.** No source cited here states the priority of generation over ancestry as a principle of harmony. The book builds on it openly. Hauptmann and Mathieu may come close; check both.

## The chain of claims

The argument as a chain, each claim tagged by what it rests on. *Premise*: the book's stance, stated openly, no citation claimed. *Supported*: rests on cited work. *Derived*: follows from earlier claims. *Hypothesis*: stated, to be tested. *Open*: not resolved.

1. **Generation before ancestry.** *Premise* (above).
2. **The prime ladder.** 2 gives identity (the octave), 3 generation (fifths), 5 color (thirds). *Supported:* Mathieu, *Harmonic Experience*; Hauptmann is close (octave as unity, fifth as duality, third as their union), and Levy's senarius uses the same three primes.
3. **The unit is the fifth.** The perfect fifth is the first relation that generates a new pitch, so the book treats it as one object with two poles: the lower pole generates, the upper pole is generated. *Premise:* a choice of unit, argued from claims 1 and 2, not proven.
4. **Addresses.** Every pitch has an address, its power of 3 and its power of 5, octaves set aside. C sits at the origin because the anchor is placed there. Addresses name relationships, not tunings. *Derived.*
5. **The mirror.** A half-turn around the center of C–G. Twins' addresses sum to (1, 0): the 5 number flips sign, the 3 numbers balance on one fifth. It leaves two sets in place: C–G (rest) and B–D–F–A♭ (tension). *Derived;* matches negative harmony in practice. An affirmation, not a proof.
6. **Major and minor are the two polar triads.** The unit plus a third hung up from the lower pole, or down from the upper. Both use the same two color intervals (4:5 and 5:6) in opposite order, Zarlino's two divisions of the fifth. Read by generation: major is one generator, two offspring (C sounds E and G); minor is two generators, one shared offspring (C and E♭ both sound G). *Derived* from 1 and 3.
7. **The root follows generation, not the mirror.** Listeners hear C as the root of C minor (*Supported*). The premise says why: the root is the generator of the fifth, and gravity traces back to it. Color mirrors; gravity does not. This answers the dualist root problem without giving up the mirror. *Derived.* The mechanism, the missing fundamental, is established for single tones; its extension to chord roots is a published model (Terhardt, Parncutt): *Hypothesis.*
8. **Home is the best-supported root.** Gravity is the tendency to hear a root when the overtone clues point to one. Each sounding pitch points to roots below it; after the octave, the strongest pointer is the fifth below. Home is the root the sounding pitches support most, and the chain stops where support runs out. *Hypothesis* (see Open questions: where gravity stops). The slope represents this tendency; it is not its cause.
9. **Windows and modes.** A window is a run of consecutive fifths, counted by its notes. Windows grown up from C and down from G pair as mirrors: the two pentatonics, Lydian and Phrygian. Six of the seven modes pair off; Locrian is the window whose root lacks its fifth (from 1 and 3). *Derived.*
10. **Tension sits at the edges of the keys.** C major (F to B) and C minor (A♭ to D) have the tritones F–B and A♭–D at their edges; together they make B°7, the mirror's fixed tension. Each resolves crossed, one voice to a pole and one to the color: B–F to C–E, A♭–D to G–E♭. *Derived.*
11. **Each anchor carries its own tension.** The anchors F–C, C–G and G–D are each a polar unit with its own mirror, so each holds its own diminished seventh: C♯°7, B°7 and C°7. Home's is the inside one; the subdominant and dominant sides give the two outside ones (see The Three Diminished Sevenths). *Derived* for the structure; *Hypothesis* for the reading as subdominant- and dominant-side energies.

## Framework at a glance

The prime ladder, five principles (plus a sixth to verify), five build steps and three dimensions hold the whole theory. Every chapter rests on these.

### Key terms

- **Dyad:** a perfect fifth treated as one polar unit, with a lower and an upper pole.
- **Lower pole / upper pole:** the root and fifth of the anchor dyad (C and G).
- **Spine:** the chain of dyads along the fifths.
- **Anchor:** the dyad currently acting as home.
- **Offspring / ancestor:** what a pitch sounds as an overtone / what the ear infers sounded it (see the premise and Coordinates).
- **Window:** a scale or mode seen as a run of consecutive fifths, counted by its notes (a seven-note window spans six fifths).
- **Region:** a key seen as a shape in the lattice, built from anchors with thirds as roofs or floors.
- **Polar triad:** a perfect fifth with a third hung up (bright) or down (dark); the only two triads that contain the unit.
- **Color:** bright or dark polarity, the sign of the 5 number (thirds, Y).
- **Brightness:** a window's lean along the spine (fifths, X), as in the brightness ladder. Related to color, not the same: Lydian is the brightest window but has no color in the 5 sense.
- **Gravity:** the tendency of a fifth to fall to its root.
- **Altitude:** height on the slope: a visual cue for gravity's direction, not a measured energy.
- **Anchorless mode:** a mode whose root has no perfect fifth within its own collection, so it lacks the complete unit (Locrian).
- **Anchorless chord:** a chord whose bottom note lacks its fifth above. Two kinds: *rootless* (augmented, diminished: no perfect fifth anywhere) and *re-rooted* (m7♭5: a fifth elsewhere that the ear may take as root).
- **Down-dominant:** a dominant built downward, named from its top note. The down-dominant from C (C A♭ F D) is predicted to be heard as Fm6.

### The prime ladder

Each step up in numerical complexity opens a new dimension of harmony. The ladder comes from Mathieu's *Harmonic Experience*; this book builds its geometry on it.

| Rung | Role | In the model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Fundamental | The origin; the source gravity points back to |
| 2 | Identity | Octave equivalence; a pitch keeps its name |
| 3 | Generation | The spine of fifths (X) |
| 5 | Color | The bright and dark layers (Y) |

The ladder gives every pitch an **address**, its power of 3 and its power of 5 (see Coordinates). Minor needs no undertone series: it is a downward step in the 5 dimension.

### Coordinates

Every pitch has an address: its power of 3 and its power of 5, with octaves set aside. The first number is generation (positive leans sharp, negative flat); the second is color (positive bright, negative dark). The lower pole, C, sits at the origin, because home is where gravity points.

**Ratios name relationships, not tunings.** Addresses and ratios record how pitches are related, not how an instrument must be tuned. The ear fills in gaps and tempers toward the nearest category; equal temperament builds that generalization into the instrument. Once the commas vanish, one key can carry several addresses, and the ear chooses among them by context (see the open question on re-addressing). The book's claims rest on which relationship holds, generation by 3 or color by 5, not on exact cents.

**Ancestor and offspring, defined.** A pitch's ancestors are the pitches that would sound it as an overtone. By generation (the 3rd partial), F is C's ancestor, as C is G's. By color (the 5th partial), C is E's ancestor, and E♭ is G's. Color ancestry is not a new principle, only the same overtone relation at the next rung. Ancestry is relative to a note, not to home: the ancestors of home lie below it, but an ancestor of the upper pole, like E♭, can fall inside the dyad, placed there by octave identity (rung 2). Nothing physical sounds below. Ancestry is the virtual fundamental the ear searches for, not an undertone series.

| Pair | Addresses | Sum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C, G | (0, 0) + (1, 0) | (1, 0) |
| F, D | (−1, 0) + (2, 0) | (1, 0) |
| E, E♭ | (0, 1) + (1, −1) | (1, 0) |
| B, A♭ | (1, 1) + (0, −1) | (1, 0) |
| A, B♭ | (−1, 1) + (2, −1) | (1, 0) |

**The mirror: twins add up to the fifth.** Each row pairs a note with its mirror twin, and every pair adds up to (1, 0): G, the anchor fifth itself. C stays home at the origin, and every number is a whole number. Read the two numbers separately and they state the theory:

- **Color is polar.** The second numbers are equal and opposite: +1 and −1, bright and dark.
- **Generation balances on the unit.** The first numbers always add up to 1, one fifth.

This is an affirmation, not a proof: the mirror musicians actually use becomes simplest when it is centered on the fifth. One coordinate system holds both worlds. Nothing is remapped between major and minor; the mirror is a rule about pairs, not a second origin. On the keyboard the same idea appears in negative harmony, where mirror pairs add up to 7 semitones (C + G, E + E♭, D + F). The lattice splits color out as its own coordinate, which equal temperament hides.

**Intervals are vectors.** A step is the difference between two addresses, so it is the same wherever it starts:

| Step | Ratio | Vector | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fifth | 3/2 | (1, 0) | C to G |
| Major third | 5/4 | (0, 1) | C to E |
| Minor third | 6/5 | (1, −1) | C to E♭ |
| Long whole step | 9/8 | (2, 0) | C to D |
| Short whole step | 10/9 | (−2, 1) | G to A |
| Diatonic semitone | 16/15 | (−1, −1) | B up to C |
| Chromatic semitone | 25/24 | (−1, 2) | E♭ up to E |

The mirror reverses every vector. B up to C, (−1, −1), becomes A♭ down to G, (+1, +1): the crossed resolutions of B°7 as one vector and its reverse. The long and short whole steps differ by the syntonic comma, the same gap as the two Ds.

**Steps cross edges.** Drawn on the triangle lattice, the three smallest steps each cross one triangle edge at a right angle, through its midpoint. The diatonic semitone crosses a minor-third edge, hopping between neighboring major-third lines (B to C crosses E–G). The short whole step crosses a major-third edge, hopping between minor-third lines. The chromatic semitone crosses a fifth edge, the color flip from E♭ to E. Harmony lives on the triangles; melodic steps live on the crossings between them. These are the single-voice moves that neo-Riemannian theory calls L, R and P.

**What the coordinates separate.** Color sits in the second number, polar. Generation sits in the first, balanced on the fifth. Gravity sits at the origin, C, in both worlds. Riemann needed two origins, one per mode, and tied the root to the symmetry, which made G the root of C minor. Here one system holds the symmetry and leaves the root with gravity.

**For computation: centered coordinates.** Doubling the generation number and centering it on the dyad (2a − 1) makes twins exact negatives: C is (−1, 0), G (1, 0), E (−1, 1), E♭ (1, −1). Every pitch then has an odd generation number, and every even number is the center of a fifth: C–G at 0, G–D at 2, F–C at −2. Useful for calculation; the book uses the C-origin form.

### Scales are readouts, not sources

The framework does not derive scales. It derives positions and energies on the lattice: anchors, colors, regions and the pull toward home. A scale is a readout of that structure, flattened into a line of steps, tempered into equal semitones and named by convention.

- **Work on the lattice first, then read out.** Mirrors, gravity and resolution happen on the lattice; the scale is what players carry to the instrument.
- **Readouts can mislead.** Mirroring a readout can land a note on the right key but the wrong address. The altered scale's mirrored C♭ is heard in C as the leading tone B, a diesis away on the lattice. Where readout and lattice disagree, the lattice and the anchor decide.
- **Conventional names describe readouts.** "Seventh mode of A♭ melodic minor" names a readout correctly and hides the structure. The book keeps the names players use and shows what sits underneath them.

This is a working principle to sharpen; see the open question on re-addressing by context.

### Principles

1. **The unit is the polar dyad.** A pitch name gives a location; its meaning comes from its position relative to the anchor dyad.
2. **The path matters.** The same pitch reached by different routes carries different color. Equal temperament hides this; the commas reveal it.
3. **Color is polar.** Bright and dark are mirror images across the anchor dyad.
4. **Gravity, represented by the slope.** The spine is generated upward from the fundamental, so every fifth tends to fall back to its generator, its root (D to G, G to C). Downward is the ancestral direction: back toward the source. The anchor marks which root is home. Color mirrors; gravity points one way: polar, not equal. The mirror derives the shapes; the ear hears the root at the bottom of the perfect fifth. Home is the best-supported root: gravity follows the overtone clues actually sounding, and the chain stops where support runs out (claim 8). The slope represents this tendency; it is not its cause.
5. **The anchor moves; the lattice doesn't.** Modulation relocates the center. Every anchor opens onto a floor of the same shape, so zooming into the new anchor shows the same lattice, relabeled and retuned.
6. **Energy lives at the edges** *(candidate, to verify)*. A window's boundary carries its energy. A window's size sets the interval at its edges and the energy it carries; its placement around the anchor sets its color. Resolution moves inward. The edge table is under Windows.

**Why color can mirror (principle 3).** The bright triad C–E–G already holds both color intervals the overtone series gives: the major third C–E (4:5) and the minor third E–G (5:6). The dark triad C–E♭–G uses the same two intervals in the opposite order. The dark color adds no new interval, only a mirrored arrangement of intervals the series already supplies: Zarlino's two divisions of the fifth, given a reason. Read through ancestry, the polarity is exact:

- **Major: one ancestor, two offspring.** C sounds both E and G.
- **Minor: two ancestors, one offspring.** G is sounded by both C and E♭.

This also explains why minor fuses less. Its shared offspring rests partly on the 5th partial, weaker than the 3rd, and the common fundamental of all three notes (10:12:15) is a distant A♭. The ear instead roots minor at the bottom of its fifth, the stronger link (principle 4).

### Windows: the reach of the spine

A window is a run of consecutive fifths on the spine. It shows how far a home reaches along the line: every note is a related tonal center, linked to its neighbors by the unit, so a window projects a family of related homes down the spine.

- **What makes a window.** No gaps. Every interior note has its ancestor and its offspring inside the window; only the two edges are incomplete. The bottom edge's ancestor lies outside, and the top edge's offspring lies outside. Collections with gaps on the spine are not windows: harmonic and melodic minor, whole-tone, diminished and augmented collections.
- **Why the edges matter.** The edges are where the reach stops, the only incomplete relationships in the window, so its energy concentrates there. The top edge cannot ground itself inside the window; in a seven-note window it is the anchorless note.
- **Two properties.** Size sets the edge interval and its energy. Placement around the anchor sets the color.

**Size sets the energy** *(candidate)*

| Pitch window | Edge span | Edge interval | Bright edges | Dark edges | Energy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 1 fifth | Perfect fifth | C–G | C–G | Rest |
| 3 | 2 fifths | Whole step | C–D | F–G | Function (to define): with home at the center, the edges are IV and V |
| 4 | 3 fifths | Minor third / major sixth | C–A | B♭–G | Relative major and minor |
| 5 | 4 fifths | Major third | C–E | E♭–G | Color |
| 6 | 5 fifths | Semitone / major seventh | C–B | G–A♭ | Leading tone |
| 7 | 6 fifths | Tritone | F–B | A♭–D | Tension |

From 2 to 7, each size introduces one new interval class at its edges, every class exactly once. The ordering is circle-of-fifths knowledge; reading each edge as an energy is what the polar unit adds.

**Arrival: the order in which color enters** *(candidate)*

Read a window together with the colors its dyads carry, and the dark degrees arrive in a fixed order. Each arrives when it becomes the color of a dyad inside the window; one also has support on the spine.

| Window | New dark degree | How it arrives | Support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C–G | ♭3 (E♭) | The dyad's own color: a major third under G, a minor third over C | Color, both poles |
| C–G–D | ♭7 (B♭) | Color of G–D, a minor third over G; the spine adds the whole step and its inversion, the minor seventh | Spine and color |
| F–C–G | ♭6 (A♭) | Color of F–C, a major third under C | Color only |
| B♭–F–C–G | ♭2 (D♭) | Color of B♭–F, a major third under F | Color only; the semitone reaches the spine only at six notes |

- **The criterion is spine support over home.** An interval somewhere in the window does not count: the dyad with E♭ already holds a minor sixth (G up to E♭), but over G, as an inverted color.
- **The mirror holds.** The bright side arrives E, A, B, F♯ (3, 6, 7, ♯4), the twins of ♭3, ♭7, ♭6, ♭2. A, like B♭, has both supports.
- **Arrival and darkness.** Listeners describe ♭7 as the lightest flat, then ♭3, ♭6 and ♭2 (practitioner reports; Huron and Davis show that lowering 3 and 6 shrinks melodic intervals, a sad cue, not that listeners rate those degrees darkest). Arrival puts ♭3 first because it belongs to the unit itself; ♭7 sounds lighter because it is the only flat the spine also supports. ♭7 and ♭3 color dyads at or above home; ♭6 and ♭2 color dyads below it, and these two are the ones never heard as bright. A hypothesis to test.
- **Two routes, one key.** The spine's B♭ (16/9) and the color B♭ (9/5) are one key in equal temperament. Read them as two reasons to hear B♭, not two tunings.

**Placement sets the color**

Slide a window along the spine and its color changes with its position relative to C–G. Three placements are fundamental:

| Size | Grown up from C (bright) | Grown down from G (dark) | Centered on C–G (neutral) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | C G | C G | C G |
| 3 | C G D | F C G | — |
| 4 | C G D A | B♭ F C G | F C G D |
| 5 | C G D A E (major pentatonic) | E♭ B♭ F C G (minor pentatonic) | — |
| 6 | C G D A E B | A♭ E♭ B♭ F C G | B♭ F C G D A |
| 7 | C G D A E B F♯ (Lydian) | D♭ A♭ E♭ B♭ F C G (Phrygian) | — |

- **Grown windows come in mirror pairs,** bright and dark, each beginning by crossing the dyad (Ch. 2).
- **Centered windows exist only at even sizes.** They are their own mirror and stay colorless: F C G D (edges D–F, the central third of B°7) and B♭ F C G D A (edges B♭–A, the signature notes of Dorian and Mixolydian).
- **Placements in between** give the modal ladder, the parallel view of the modes (Ch. 3).

**The key collections.** C major (F C G D A E B) and C minor (A♭ E♭ B♭ F C G D) are seven-note windows placed one step in from the grown ones, so all three function anchors (F–C, C–G, G–D) sit inside.

- Their edges are the two tritones, F–B and A♭–D. Together they form B°7, the mirror's fixed tension.
- The edges resolve inward to the five-note edges: B–F to C–E, A♭–D to G–E♭.
- The top edges, B and D, are the anchorless notes (Ch. 9).

**Anchor windows.** Counting anchors instead of pitches, n anchors span n + 1 pitches. The three anchors F–C, C–G and G–D are the roots of IV I V and form a centered window, its own mirror: IV I V and iv i v stand on the same anchors, with thirds hung up or down. With thirds added, they build C major and C minor (see Regions).

### The build

1. **Line (X):** fifths alone. The dyad, the pentatonics, the modes.
2. **Plane (X + Y):** thirds add polarity. The lattice, color, the mirror.
3. **Slope (Z):** tilt the plane. Gravity as falling fifths; the sharp side is brighter, higher and more tense.
4. **Circle:** pitch names repeat every twelve fifths, so the spine wants to close.
5. **Helix:** roll the gently tilted floor into a cylinder, one column per key. Twelve fifths return to C's column one comma higher, so the circle becomes a helix of anchors, each opening onto its own floor.

X and Y are the two numbers of a pitch's address (see Coordinates): X its power of 3, Y its power of 5. Z is not a third address number; it is a gentle tilt along X, a visual cue for gravity's direction. Rolled into a cylinder, it becomes the helix (Ch. 10).

### The mirror and its fixed objects

The mirror is a half-turn around the center of the anchor dyad. Its two key fixed objects are the dyad C–G (rest) and the diminished seventh B–D–F–A♭ (tension). Other sets map onto themselves too, such as the symmetric windows F C G D and B♭ F C G D A, but these two carry the system's rest and tension.

### Part I: Generation (the line, fifths only)

**From One** *Claim: harmony begins where the fifth produces something the octave cannot.*

- 1:1, the unison: the fundamental alone.
- 2:1, the octave: the first step away, but it returns the same pitch. Identity.
- 3:2, the fifth: the first relationship that generates a new pitch. Harmony begins here. The fifth also implies its origin: as harmonics 2 and 3, it points the ear back to the fundamental beneath it.
- ◆ The ladder 1, 2, 3, 5, with the fifth as the first new sound.

**1. The Dyad** *Claim: C–G is one object with two poles, and the only fifth the mirror leaves in place.*

- The spine as a chain of dyads; the mirror swaps them in pairs.
- ◆ The dyad chain, mirror pairs linked by arcs across C–G.

**2. Growing in Two Directions** *Claim: major and minor pentatonic are one act of growth, begun from opposite poles.*

- Major pentatonic grows up from C: C → G → D → A → E.
- Minor pentatonic grows down from G: G → C → F → B♭ → E♭.
- Each begins by crossing the dyad, then keeps going.
- The inner, thirdless pair (C D F G A and C D F G B♭) centers on the two poles: color-free, neutral.
- Pentatonics across West African, East Asian, Celtic, Andean, Native American, blues and folk traditions: widespread and foundational.
- ◆◆ Growing the pentatonic: two arrows leaving the dyad from opposite poles.

**3. Seven-Note Windows: The Modes** *Claim: the modes are windows on the spine, and they pair off as mirrors.*

- Lydian ↔ Phrygian, Ionian ↔ Aeolian, Mixolydian ↔ Dorian.
- **The Twin Palindromes:** Dorian centered on C, Mixolydian on G, both W H W W W H W. Signature notes A and B♭ are a mirror pair. Dorian i–IV mirrors Mixolydian I–v; Mixolydian I–♭VII–IV mirrors Dorian i–ii–v.
- Brightness as distance from the dyad; Temperley and Tan's mode ratings follow the spine except at Lydian, which they attribute mainly to familiarity.
- Seven notes bring the tritone: tension enters. The semitone arrives one step earlier, at six notes (see the nested windows).
- ◆ The brightness ladder: six modes as bands along the spine.
- ◆ Twin palindromes, each with its center pole lit.

**Two views of the modes** *(to organize)*. Both are useful mental models, and the book needs both:

- **Parallel, one root:** the anchor stays fixed on C–G and the window slides along the spine. This gives the brightness ladder and the mirror pairs (Ch. 3).

In either view, the anchorless note is the top edge of its window.

### Part II: Color (the plane, thirds enter)

*Opening: 5 enters. Color is the sign of the 5: a positive power is bright, a negative power dark.*

**4. The Second Path** *Claim: the same note reached by fifths or by a third is not the same note.*

- Four fifths reach E; one major third reaches a different E. The syntonic comma.
- The bright third pulls its note down toward the anchor; the dark third pulls its note up, by the same amount.
- ◆ Two roads to E, the comma gap marked.

**5. The Lattice and the Turn** *Claim: the mirror is a half-turn of the triangle lattice (Mathieu's lattice with each third over the middle of its fifth) around the center of C–G.*

- Spine in the middle, bright layer above, dark layer below.
- Major hangs a third up from C; minor hangs a third down from G.
- **Two polar triads.** A triad is the unit plus a color, so there are exactly two: bright (a third hung up from the lower pole) and dark (a third hung down from the upper pole). They are the only triads that are triangles in the lattice. Every chord without a perfect fifth is anchorless.
- E ↔ E♭, B ↔ A♭, A ↔ B♭, F♯ ↔ D♭, F ↔ D.
- ◆◆ The Mirror Machine: the lattice rotating 180°, carrying any chord or scale to its twin.

**Regions** *Claim: a key is a shape in the lattice, not a run of fifths, and function lives in the geometry.*

- Windows run along the spine (Part I). Regions are shapes in the plane, once thirds enter.
- **C major:** the spine F C G D with A, E and B as roofs over F–C, C–G and G–D. IV, I and V point up; vi and iii point down between them. **C minor:** the same spine with A♭, E♭ and B♭ as floors. iv, i and v point down; VI and III point up between them. The mirror flips roofs to floors.
- **A region is a different C major.** The window reaches E by four fifths (Pythagorean); the region reaches it by one third (just intonation). Same names, different addresses, a syntonic comma apart: principle 2 made visible.
- **Two Ds.** ii is the down-triangle just left of IV. Its D is a major third above B♭ (10/9), on the predominant side, derived from a note outside the collection. V's D sits on the spine at the right (9/8), on the dominant side. They are a syntonic comma apart. A pure ii–V–I must move D by a comma (the comma pump); equal temperament merges the two. The lattice places ii on the predominant side by its derivation, not by convention.
- **The mirror of ii.** Flipped across C–G, C major's ii becomes ♭VII (B♭ major) in C minor, just right of v, on the dominant side: the same pairing as Dorian ii and Mixolydian ♭VII.
- **The hexagon.** Parallel C major and C minor together form a hexagon centered on C–G. Interior: C, G, E and E♭, the two tonic triads. Corners: F, D, B and A♭ (B°7, the tension) and A and B♭ (the Dorian and Mixolydian signature pair). The half-turn swaps opposite corners.

### Part III: Forces (the slope)

**6. The Slope** *Claim: tilting the plane turns the fifths into gravity.*

- G sits above C and falls to it; every fifth falls to its root.
- Descending-fifth motion: ii–V–I and every circle progression.
- The sharp side is brighter, higher and more tense; the flat side darker and lower. Whether it is also more relaxed than home depends on where gravity stops (open question).
- ◆ The tilted lattice with falling-fifth arrows.

**7. The Two Dominants** *Claim: rest and tension are the mirror's two key fixed objects.*

- G7 built up from the upper pole; its mirror is the down-dominant built from the lower pole: C A♭ F D. Spelled from the bottom it looks like Dm7♭5, but its only perfect fifth is F–C, so gravity should make F the heard root: Fm6 (a prediction, to test). G is the dominant above, F the dominant below.
- **Which F is the seventh?** G7 is the G triad (the G–D anchor with B as its roof) plus the ancestral F, a fifth below C on the spine. That F is the hexagon's corner, so B–F runs corner to corner as the bright half of B°7, and both tritone voices resolve by the same pure semitone (16/15): B up into C, F down into E. The compact tertian shape, with F a minor third above D (27/20), sits outside the region, a comma higher, and breaks that balance. The dominant is the triad above with the added energy of the ancestral F, pointing home from both sides. Mirror: the iv triad plus the spine D, a fifth above G. To test: whether pure-tuning ears prefer the 10/9 D in that Fm6. (7-limit sevenths set aside for now.)
- Together they make B°7, unchanged by the mirror.
- Crossed resolutions: the bright tritone B–F resolves to C–E (B up to C, F down to E), the lower pole with its bright third. The dark tritone A♭–D resolves to G–E♭ (A♭ down to G, D up to E♭), the upper pole with its dark third. Each tritone can close (as a diminished fifth) or open (as an augmented fourth). In every case one voice lands on a pole and the other lands on the color.
- Modes as slices of B°7: Ionian B D F ↔ Aeolian D F A♭; Lydian B D ↔ Phrygian F A♭; Mixolydian and Dorian share D F.
- ◆ The tension cross: B°7 with arrows into both poles.

**8. Gravity: Polar, Not Equal** *Claim: the structure is symmetric; the pull is not.*

- The fifth pulls to its root whatever the color. Listeners hear C as root of C minor. The missing-fundamental effect explains why: the minor triad implies no clear fundamental of its own, so the ear takes its root from the fifth inside it (see the thesis).
- This answers the dualist objection without giving up the mirror.
- Stability and brightness as two measurable listening dimensions (Krumhansl; Temperley and Tan).
- ◆ A symmetric color field with gravity pointing to C.

**9. Locrian, the Anchorless Mode** *Claim: Locrian is the one mode that does not contain the complete unit, so it is inherently different.*

- The no-sharps, no-flats collection is a seven-note window: F C G D A E B. Every note but B has its fifth above inside the window. B's fifth, F♯, lies outside: to ground B you must leave the key signature.
- In a system whose unit is the fifth, Locrian is anchorless: its root has no polar unit within its own collection. The other six modes each contain their anchor dyad.
- Lydian sits at the opposite edge but keeps its anchor. F lacks the fifth below (B♭), its ancestor, which only removes the subdominant pull; it still generates C. Locrian lacks its offspring, the fifth that grounds it. Polar, not equal.
- This replaces "it lacks a fifth": the issue is not a missing interval but a missing unit. Why it's still worth loving: color without ground.
- **Anchorless chords are re-rooted by gravity** *(prediction, to test)*. A m7♭5 has no perfect fifth on its bottom note, so the ear takes its root from the fifth it does contain: Bø7 (B D F A) should be heard as Dm6, and C A♭ F D as Fm6 (Ch. 7).
- ◆ The seven-fifth window, with B's fifth falling outside and F's anchor intact.

**Negative space: the anchorless mode in C minor.** The mirror of the bright collection (F C G D A E B) is the dark one (A♭ E♭ B♭ F C G D). Structurally, B Locrian's twin is A♭, the last note generated building down from D. But the ear grounds A♭ by its fifth above (E♭), so it sounds as A♭ Lydian. D is the note whose upward fifth (A) falls outside: D is anchorless. The mirror swaps the roles of the two edges, and gravity decides which edge is anchorless: always the top.

|  | Grounded edge (Lydian) | Anchorless edge |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bright: C major | F | B |
| Dark: C minor | A♭ | D |

- The anchorless note is always the 7th of the collection's major scale, the top of its fifths window: B in C, D in E♭, the relative major of C minor.
- The two anchorless chords pull into opposite poles: Bø7 (viiø7 in C) leads to C; Dø7 (iiø7 in C minor) leads to G.
- **The edges are the tension.** Each collection's two edges form its tritone: F–B and A♭–D. All four edges together are B–D–F–A♭, the diminished seventh the mirror leaves unchanged (Ch. 7). The complete tension of parallel C major and C minor lives at the edges of the two windows.

### Part IV: Landscape (the circle and the helix)

**10. The Circle That Can't Close** *Claim: a slope cannot close into a circle; it becomes a helix.*

- Roll the gently tilted floor into a cylinder, one column per key. Twelve fifths return to C's column one comma higher (about 23.5 cents): the circle becomes a helix.
- Height on the cylinder is distance from equal temperament: each pure fifth climbs about 2 cents. The two Es stack about 21.5 cents apart in E's column (the syntonic comma); color shows as tint here, since just thirds run against it in height. Equal temperament flattens every column to a point and closes the circle.
- ◆◆ Helix and Zoom: the helix of anchors as overview, one comma per lap; zoom into any anchor to see its own floor.

**11. F♯ and G♭** *Claim: enharmonic notes are bright and dark because they are derived in opposite directions.*

- Six fifths up versus six fifths down: same key, opposite levels.
- Spelling and expressive intonation.
- ◆ The enharmonic pair on two levels of the helix.

**12. Moving the Anchor** *Claim: modulation moves the origin, not the lattice.*

- Every anchor opens onto a floor of the same shape, relabeled and retuned. Modulation moves the zoom along the helix; commas appear only where two anchors' floors overlap (the two Ds).
- ◆◆ Helix and Zoom (Ch. 10): move along the helix and zoom into the new anchor's floor.

**The other two spines.** The triangle lattice has three directions, one for each edge of a triangle: fifths, major thirds and minor thirds. Each is an open line in pure tuning; equal temperament closes all three at once.

| Spine | Helix rotation in ET | Gap in pure tuning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fifths | 12 steps | Pythagorean comma, about 23.5 cents |
| Major thirds | 3 steps (augmented) | Lesser diesis, about 41 cents |
| Minor thirds | 4 steps (diminished) | Greater diesis, about 63 cents |

Since 12 = 3 × 4, every pitch class sits on exactly one augmented triad and one diminished seventh, and the circle of fifths winds through both.

**Not polar triads.** Augmented and diminished triads contain no perfect fifth and are not triangles in the lattice. They are anchorless sonorities, built from pieces of polar triads lined up along a third spine. In harmonic minor, E♭ G B is E♭–G from C minor and G–B from G major, stacked by tertian spelling. Great chords, but not made of the same stuff.

**Two express lanes.** Both third spines are fast tracks for moving the anchor, with different strides along the fifths:

| Lane | Fifths per move | Notes shared between keys | Territory |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fifths | 1 | 6 | Local |
| Minor thirds | 3 | 4 | Shared: parallel and relative keys |
| Major thirds | 4 | 3 | New |

- A major third up is four fifths sharp; a minor third up is three fifths flat. Three hops of four, or four hops of three, make one lap: the rotations of 3 and 4.
- **The minor-third lane stays in the family.** Three fifths is the distance between an anchor's bright and dark collections (C major and C minor), and relative keys sit a minor third apart (C major and A minor, C minor and E♭ major). That is why diminished feels like it closes: its lane stays inside shared territory. The diminished seventh's pivot role and the axis system in Bartók analysis both treat keys a minor third apart as linked.
- **The major-third lane leaves.** Keys a major third apart share only three notes. Augmented moves open outward, usually through a raised leading tone, into new territory: chromatic mediants, and Coltrane's "Giant Steps" (B, G, E♭), a full lap of the augmented helix.

Both can be steered elsewhere; these are tendencies.

**Why the two lanes differ.** The sound of even and odd intervals points the way. Intervals that split into equal halves (a major third into two whole steps, a minor sixth into two major thirds) build the whole-tone scale and the augmented triad: no fifth anywhere, nothing to orient them. Diminished floats for a different reason: its minor thirds each carry a fifth's worth of generation, but four of them close a cycle and cancel. What each step is made of:

- **Fifth (1, 0):** pure generation. The local road, where gravity works.
- **Major third (0, 1):** pure color, no generation.
- **Minor third (1, −1):** one fifth of generation plus one step of dark color.

**The major-third lane leaves.** In equal temperament a major third up counts as four fifths (E major has four sharps). In the lattice it is one color step: the same E reached two ways, a comma apart (Ch. 4, The Second Path). So the move is harmonically one step but a long jump along the spine. That is the chromatic-mediant feeling, close through a common tone and far by key signature, with nothing generational carrying home along.

**The minor-third lane stays in the family.** Alternating P (parallel) and R (relative) walks the minor-third cycle: C, C minor, E♭, E♭ minor, G♭. Alternating P and L (leading-tone exchange) walks the major-third cycle: C, C minor, A♭, A♭ minor, E. *Supported:* standard neo-Riemannian results, developed by Cohn (octatonic and hexatonic cycles). Read in this book's terms:

- **P** flips home's color and keeps the unit, so the anchor stays.
- **R** moves to the relative and keeps the collection.
- **L** keeps neither the anchor nor the collection.

The minor-third lane is built entirely from moves that each keep something of home; the major-third lane needs a move that keeps nothing. Its first stops are the relatives of home's two colors: A minor for the bright side, E♭ major for the dark. One hop on the minor-third lane never leaves home's family; one hop on the major-third lane already has. *Derived,* using cited tools. This is the reason the lanes differ; the count of three fifths against four describes the distance, not the cause.

**The Major-Third Spine: Augmented** *Claim: the major-third spine is a helix of 3, made only of color, and it always comes in bright and dark pairs.*

- Three pure major thirds fall short of the octave by the lesser diesis: C, E, G♯ and B♯ lands just below C. A rotation of 3.
- Each step is a power of 5 alone, so the spine is pure color, with no fifth to ground it.
- Anchorless: no perfect fifth, so gravity cannot choose a root, and any note can act as root.
- Never fixed by a fifth-anchor mirror: it always flips to a different augmented triad. C E G♯ built up from C mirrors to G E♭ C♭ built down from G. Harmonic major's A♭ C E mirrors to harmonic minor's E♭ G B.
- ◆ The major-third helix: three steps per lap, the diesis gap marked.

**The Minor-Third Spine: Diminished** *Claim: the minor-third spine is a helix of 4, and its collection is the mirror's fixed tension.*

- Four pure minor thirds overshoot the octave by the greater diesis. A rotation of 4.
- Each minor third mixes both dimensions (6/5: one fifth up, one third down), so the spine runs diagonally between generation and color.
- Anchorless: no perfect fifth, so the root is ambiguous.
- Fixed by the mirrors of four anchors a minor third apart: C–G, E♭–B♭, F♯–C♯ and A–E. One tension object shared by four homes, which is why it pivots to four keys.
- In pure tuning the functional B°7 is not a straight line: two pure minor thirds (B–D from V's side, F–A♭ from iv's side) joined by the spine third D–F. The mirror swaps the outer two and keeps D–F.
- ◆ The minor-third helix: four steps per lap, B°7 shared by four anchors.

**The Three Diminished Sevenths** *Claim: every source of a mirror (a pole, a color tone, the unit, an anchor) holds or trades minor-third lines, so the three diminished sevenths are home's tension and the tensions of the anchors beside it.*

Two kinds of source generate pitch sets by mirroring. The polar unit is a pair, so its mirror is pair-based. A single pitch (a pole, or one of the unit's color tones) gives a tone-based mirror. This section works in lattice addresses throughout; twelve-tone sums appear in parentheses as readouts.

**Notation: the lattice sum.** For any mirror, add the addresses of any pair of twins: the result is always the same vector, S = (s3, s5), twice the mirror's center. This is the rule that twins add up to (1, 0), applied to every mirror. The twelve-tone sum used in negative harmony and in Coleman's spirals is a readout of S: 7·s3 + 4·s5 (mod 12), since a fifth is 7 semitones and a major third 4. Many lattice sums read out to the same number, differing by commas and dieses, which is what equal temperament erases. The twelve-tone sum names a key-level mirror; S names its address.

- **s3, the generation sum.** Its parity is the parity of the twelve-tone sum, so the odd/even distinction survives the lift exactly. Odd: the center sits halfway along a fifth (pair-based). Even: it sits on a tone (tone-based).
- **s5, the color sum.** Zero: the center is on the spine, and the mirror flips bright and dark around it. Nonzero: the center sits on a color layer.

| Mirror | Center | Lattice sum S | ET sum | Kind | Line of centers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pole F | F | (−2, 0) | 10 | tone | spine |
| Anchor F–C | middle of F–C | (−1, 0) | 5 | pair | spine |
| Pole C | C | (0, 0) | 0 | tone | spine |
| Unit C–G | middle of C–G | (1, 0) | 7 | pair | spine and chromatic semitone |
| Pole G | G | (2, 0) | 2 | tone | spine |
| Anchor G–D | middle of G–D | (3, 0) | 9 | pair | spine |
| Color tone E | E | (0, 2) | 8 | tone | chromatic semitone |
| Color tone E♭ | E♭ | (2, −2) | 6 | tone | chromatic semitone |

**Two lines of centers cross at the unit.** Along the spine, centers step by half a fifth: poles on tones, the unit and the anchors on the midpoints between them (each step adds 7 to the twelve-tone sum). Through the same center runs a second line: from the unit, S changes by (−1, 2) to reach E and by (1, −2) to reach E♭. That vector is the chromatic semitone, E♭ to E, the P move. So the color-tone spirals (8 and 6) are not an equal-temperament artifact: they run along the color-flip direction, while the poles and anchors run along the generation direction. In twelve-tone notation both look like neighboring sums; the lattice shows they are different directions.

**From the unit: (1, 0), sum 7.** The spiral starts at the unit's own chromatic pair, E♭–E, and runs out through D–F, D♭–F♯, C–G and B–A♭ to the second half-step pair, A–B♭. Crossing the two half-step pairs gives the border tritones E–B♭ and E♭–A.

**From the color tones: (0, 2) and (2, −2), sums 8 and 6.** Taken one at a time, each color tone starts a tone-based spiral.

- **From E (sum 8):** E–E, E♭–F, D–F♯, D♭–G, C–G♯, B–A, B♭–B♭. In equal temperament E–B♭ is held in place and D♭–G swapped.
- **From E♭ (sum 6):** E♭–E♭, D–E, D♭–F, C–F♯, B–G, B♭–A♭, A–A. E♭–A is held; C–F♯ swapped.

The border tritones of the unit's spiral are the axes of these two: the unit's mirror exchanges the two color-tone mirrors.

**From the poles: (0, 0) and (2, 0), the minor-third spines.** Each pole runs its minor-third spine both ways. Down a minor third adds (−1, 1), bright; up adds (1, −1), dark.

- **C:** C A F♯ (bright) and C E♭ G♭ (dark).
- **G:** G E C♯ (bright) and G B♭ D♭ (dark).
- **F, the ancestor, (−2, 0):** F D B (bright) and F A♭ C♭ (dark).

Each spine is symmetric under the half-turn around its pole. The first ring around each pole is a border tritone: A and E♭ around C, E and B♭ around G.

**Diminished sevenths are minor-third lines.** A minor third, (1, −1), leaves a + b unchanged, so the lattice's minor-third lines are the lines where a + b is constant. A diminished seventh in equal temperament is the family of these lines with the same a + b (mod 3), merged. A mirror maps a + b to (s3 + s5) − (a + b), so it holds a line exactly when s3 + s5 is even, and trades lines in pairs when it is odd.

| Mirror | s3 + s5 | Minor-third lines |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pole C (0, 0); color tone E♭ (2, −2) | 0 | Both hold C's line (C A F♯ / C E♭ G♭), centered at C and at E♭ |
| Pole G (2, 0); color tone E (0, 2) | 2 | Both hold G's line (G E C♯ / G B♭ D♭), centered at G and at E |
| Pole F (−2, 0) | −2 | Holds F's line (F D B / F A♭ C♭) |
| Unit C–G (1, 0) | 1 | Holds none: trades C's line with G's line, and the segment B–D with F–A♭ |

So the color tone and the pole agree exactly in the lattice: each pair is one half-turn family on one minor-third line, centered at different notes of it. E sits one minor third below G on G's line; E♭ one minor third above C on C's line. Note the crossing: the bright tone goes with the upper pole, the dark tone with the lower. And the twelve-tone statement "sum 7 holds B°7" lifts to something sharper: the unit's mirror trades two parallel minor-third segments, the bright B–D and the dark F–A♭. That is the hexagon's B°7 and its bright and dark tritones. Coverage of all twelve notes by three diminished sevenths is arithmetic; the line structure is what the lattice adds.

**Three species of tritone.** Add a tritone's two hexagon addresses and read the color number. F–B and C–F♯ are bright; A♭–D and D♭–G are dark. E–B♭ gives (2, 0) and E♭–A gives (0, 0), so their color cancels: each joins one bright tone to one dark one. The neutral pair splits by pole instead: E–B♭ centers on G, E♭–A on C. They are the first rings of the pole spines, which is why they cancel. *Derived;* species depend on address (see What the lattice decides).

**Edges.** Slide a seven-note window past C–G. Each slide moves the edge tritone by a fifth, and 7 ≡ 1 (mod 3), so the edges cycle through the three diminished sevenths with period three.

| Mode on C | Window | Edge tritone | Color | Diminished 7th |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lydian | C … F♯ | C–F♯ | bright | C°7 |
| Ionian | F … B | F–B | bright | B°7 |
| Mixolydian | B♭ … E | E–B♭ | neutral | C♯°7 |
| Dorian | E♭ … A | E♭–A | neutral | C°7 |
| Aeolian | A♭ … D | A♭–D | dark | B°7 |
| Phrygian | D♭ … G | D♭–G | dark | C♯°7 |

- **The neutral tritones are the edges of the twin palindromes.** The centered six-note window B♭ F C G D A has B♭–A at its edges; one more note either way gives E–B♭ or E♭–A.
- **Each diminished seventh is the joint edge set of two windows three fifths apart,** one hop on the minor-third lane: Ionian and Aeolian, Mixolydian and Phrygian, Lydian and Dorian.
- **Edge color runs bright, bright, neutral, neutral, dark, dark:** placement sets color at the edges too (principle 6).
- **Six windows, three mirror pairs.** Lydian and Phrygian, Ionian and Aeolian, Mixolydian and Dorian: each tritone appears once, and each diminished seventh twice. Locrian adds no new edge. Its G♭–C is Lydian's C–F♯ reached from the other end of the spine (see The tritone as antipode).

**Resolution.** In the diatonic setting E–B♭ resolves inward to F–A (IV, bright) and E♭–A to D–B♭ (the top of v, dark); the unit's mirror swaps the two, as it swaps IV and v. B°7 resolves into the home anchor; the border tritones resolve into the flanking anchors, crossed: the bright tone's tension falls flatward to F, the dark tone's rises toward G. Each pole's spine is also the rootless 7♭9 into its offspring, with the pole as seventh (F's into C, C's into G, G's into D): the ancestral F in G7 (Ch. 7), generalized. *Hypothesis.*

**Inside and outside: one diminished seventh per anchor.** Each anchor of the window F–C, C–G, G–D is a polar unit with its own mirror: (−1, 0), (1, 0) and (3, 0), sums 5, 7 and 9. All three are pair-based and centered on the spine, the same kind as home's mirror, so each carries its own key's tension exactly as C–G carries B°7. Home's diminished seventh is the inside one; the subdominant and dominant anchors give the two outside ones.

| Subdominant | F–C | (−1, 0) (5) | E–G | B♭–D♭ | C♯°7 | F |

- **Outside means reach.** A diminished seventh resolves four ways, into the notes a half step above its members, each a hop apart on the minor-third lane. Home's B°7 reaches C, E♭, G♭ and A: home's own minor-third family, the shared territory of parallel and relative keys, which is why it feels like it closes. The outside sets reach the other two families: C°7 into G, B♭, D♭ and E (vii°7 of V), C♯°7 into F, A♭, B and D (vii°7 of IV). Together they reach every root home's tension does not, so the cadential energy of the anchors beside home tends to carry the ear further afield.
- **Same structure, transposed by the spine.** Each anchor's mirror holds no minor-third line; it trades two parallel segments, one bright and one dark, as home's mirror trades B–D and F–A♭. The tritones are the edges of that anchor's major and minor collections: F–C has E–B♭ (F major) and D♭–G (F minor); G–D has C–F♯ (G major) and E♭–A (G minor).
- **Crossed resolution on every anchor.** Subdominant side: E–B♭ to F–A, D♭–G to C–A♭. Dominant side: F♯–C to G–B, E♭–A to D–B♭. In each, one voice lands on a pole and one on the color, as B–F to C–E and A♭–D to G–E♭ at home.
- **Two routes to each tension.** The tension into a pole is held by that pole's own unit mirror and by its ancestor's minor-third spine. Into C: the C–G mirror and F's spine (B°7). Into G: the G–D mirror and C's spine (C°7). Into F: the F–C mirror and B♭'s spine (C♯°7).
- **Two routes to the outside sets.** The anchors reach the outside diminished sevenths along the spine; the color tones reach the same twelve-tone sets along the chromatic semitone, crossed: the bright tone E with the subdominant side, the dark tone E♭ with the dominant side.
- **The neutral tritones take color from a neighbor.** E–B♭ is neutral from home (E is home's bright third, B♭ the dark floor under G–D), but bright from F–C, where E is F's leading tone and B♭ F's ancestor on the spine. E♭–A is neutral from home but dark from G–D, where E♭ is G's dark color and A G's offspring on the spine. The same keys change address with the anchor heard as home, so these tritones are where home's reading and its neighbors' readings disagree.

This gives the anchor window IV I V a tension for each anchor: three units, three diminished sevenths, inside and outside. It fits the book's dominant and subdominant sides directly. *Derived* for the structure; *Hypothesis* for the reading as dominant- and subdominant-side energies.

**Blue notes on the axis.** The unit's axis crosses the chromatic circle between E♭ and E and between A and B♭. In continuous pitch its fixed points are 350 and 950 cents: the neutral third, and the zone between 6 and ♭7. The border tritones are those of I7 (C7: E–B♭) and IV7 (F7: A–E♭) in a blues, and the mirror swaps them. The harmonic seventh (7:4, about 969 cents) sits near the second fixed point, which bears on the boundary at 5 (open questions). *Hypothesis, to test.*

**What the lattice decides.**

- **Direction, not exactness.** Every mirror here is a true half-turn of the lattice. The spine-centered ones (poles, unit, anchors) flip color around the spine. The color-tone ones are centered on a color layer, so they send the spine to the doubly bright layer (E) or the doubly dark layer (E♭); they keep their own minor-third line instead.
- **The arms don't meet.** Two minor thirds down from C reach F♯ at 25/18 (569 cents); two up reach G♭ at 36/25 (631 cents), a greater diesis apart. The equal-tempered tritone, 600 cents, is their exact midpoint: the neutral tritone. Spelling records which arm a note came from.
- **Two B°7s.** F's spine gives F, D at 10/9, B at 50/27, A♭ at 8/5 and C♭ at 48/25; its D is ii's D, so it reads as predominant. The hexagon's B°7 is F, D at 9/8, B at 15/8 and A♭: V's D and the true leading tone, the dominant reading. They share only F and A♭, and A♭–D is dark in one reading and neutral in the other. Same keys, a comma apart: the two Ds (Regions) again.
- **Neutral from home, colored from a neighbor.** E–B♭ and E♭–A are neutral when read from C–G and take color from the anchors beside it (above).

**The tritone as antipode.** On a circle, every mirror has two centers half a circle apart. The unit's axis crosses the circle of fifths at C–G and again at F♯–C♯ (G♭–D♭); on the chromatic circle it crosses between E♭ and E and between A and B♭, the two blue-note points, a tritone apart. A pole's mirror holds the pole and its tritone (C and F♯). So in equal temperament everything mirrored at home is mirrored again at the tritone, and tritone transposition leaves every diminished seventh in place. B°7 is the rootless 7♭9 of both G and D♭ (the tritone substitution), so home's tension resolves to C or to its antipode, F♯, as well as along the minor-third lane.

In the lattice the spine is a line, so there is no antipode: the far point splits in two. Six fifths up from C reaches F♯, the bright extreme; six down reaches G♭, the dark extreme, a Pythagorean comma apart. Two minor thirds down and up reach F♯ and G♭ a greater diesis apart. Every such pair is centered on C and swapped exactly by C's own mirror; equal temperament merges them, so the pole's mirror appears to hold its tritone. The tritone is where the open spines meet themselves halfway around.

So there are two polarities. The unit's is local: generation and color around a fifth. The tritone's is global: the circle's antipode, real in equal temperament, and in the lattice the place the comma lands, where bright and dark extremes are merged by closure. Lydian's C–F♯ and Locrian's G♭–C are the same edge reached from opposite ends of the spine. Ties to Ch. 10 (the circle that can't close) and Ch. 11 (F♯ and G♭).

**Working rule.** Twelve-tone and chord-scale names identify keys; lattice addresses identify relationships. Every mod-12 statement is restated as a lattice statement plus the comma or diesis equal temperament erased. A diminished seventh is a key-level object that the lattice splits into versions (predominant and dominant, bright arm and dark arm, inside and outside), and the ear chooses by context.

This is where the model meets the bandstand. Scale language only names scales upward from a bottom note, so it cannot say what happens here; the polar unit can.

**Credit.** Much of this chapter stands on Levy's negative dominants (see Beside Levy and Coleman). What it adds: heard-root naming, comma placement, and the A melodic minor ↔ B♭ derivation.

**The directional premise.** The mirror derives the shape; gravity names the root. Built downward, the dark side's dominants take the half-diminished shape. Heard, the ear roots each at the bottom of its perfect fifth, so they sound as m6 chords.

**The derivation on the lattice, read out as scales**

The mirror happens on the lattice; the scales below are its readout, flattened and spelled by convention. Where the two disagree, as with C♭ heard as B, the lattice and the anchor decide.

1. **Up from C.** C–G–D–A, three fifths up. A melodic minor built upward: A B C D E F♯ G♯ (W H W W W W H).
2. **Its fourth mode is D Lydian dominant:** D E F♯ G♯ A B C (W W W H W H W up). Very bright: a secondary dominant from the key of G, with G♯ borrowed from the key of A.
3. **Down from G.** G–C–F–B♭, three fifths down. The mirror parent is B♭ melodic minor built downward: B♭ A♭ G F E♭ D♭ C♭ (W H W W W W H down).
4. **Its fourth mode down is the F scale:** F E♭ D♭ C♭ B♭ A♭ G (W W W H W H W down). Played over G, this is what players call G altered.

**What the usual names hide**

- **"Seventh mode of A♭ melodic minor."** A♭ shares the notes but has no mirror relationship to A. The true mirror is B♭, built down.
- **m7♭5 over the dominant.** Fm7♭5 or Gm7♭5 over G7 is taught as an upper-structure trick. Here they are the dark side's two dominants, built downward, and heard as B♭m6 and A♭m6. A dominant seventh turned upside down is a m7♭5: the two chords are exact inversions of each other.

**The crossed pull.** The bright dominant resolves into the upper pole (D7 to G); the dark one resolves into the lower pole (G7alt to C). That is the same crossed pattern as the two tritones of B°7. Gravity then carries the bright side one step further: G still falls to C.

**Vocabulary needed.** The book needs a short way to say "built downward from," so readers can say "F, downward" as easily as "D, upward." Naming runs from opposite ends: a down-dominant is named from its top note, a m7♭5 from its bottom note. The down-dominant from F (F D♭ B♭ G) is Gm7♭5; from E♭ (E♭ C♭ A♭ F), Fm7♭5. Heard, each is rooted by gravity at the bottom of its perfect fifth: B♭m6 and A♭m6.

### Beside Levy and Coleman: two axes, one mirror

Source: Steve Coleman's essay "Symmetrical Movement Concept," including his summary of Ernst Levy's *A Theory of Harmony*. This is Coleman's reading of Levy; verify in Levy before citing.

**Two axes.** Levy flips around a single tone, the generator: C–E–G read downward from C is C–A♭–F, F minor. The intervals match, but the heard root moves to F, so the root has to be explained afterward (telluric adaptation). This book flips around the middle of the fifth C–G, Coleman's "sum 7" axis (between E♭ and E, and between A and B♭): C–E–G becomes G–E♭–C, C minor. The fifth stays put; only the third flips. The two axes sit half a fifth apart, so their results land a fifth apart. Every flip exists in both systems; what differs is which one the theory is built on.

**Why Levy centers on a tone.** Not an oversight: it follows from his starting point. If overtones and undertones are equally real, the series is symmetric around one tone, so the center has to be a tone, the generator, with its poles the fifths on either side (F–C–G). Generation before ancestry removes that commitment. Once undertones are inference, nothing pins the center to a tone, and the first generated relation, the fifth, becomes the unit. And since telluric adaptation already explains which root we hear, the generator at the center of absolute conception does no perceptual work. This book keeps the part of Levy that matches hearing, his telluric gravity, and moves the center into the fifth. Levy described the poles; his undertone commitment put them around a tone instead of inside a fifth.

**The unit makes its own axis.** In Coleman's spirals every axis is a unison or a half step, and the middle of C–G is the half-step axis E♭–E (sum 7; spiral out and you reach D–F, D♭–F♯, then C–G). Coleman chooses an axis and spirals outward; he does not single out this one or tie it to the fifth. Here it falls out of the unit: E is generated (the 5th partial of C, offspring of the lower pole), and E♭ is inferred (G is its 5th partial, so it is the source the ear supplies under the upper pole). One color sounded up from the bottom, one implied down from the top: the premise, generation before ancestry, shows up inside the symmetry, and it is why minor fuses less. The coordinates agree: E = (0, 1) and E♭ = (1, −1) have their midpoint at (½, 0), the mirror's center. The step between them is the chromatic semitone (25/24), the P move, which equal temperament flattens into Coleman's plain half step. So the half-step axis is not an assumption here; it is the two colors the fifth creates. *Derived.*

**Two kinds of mirror, and Coleman's spirals.** Set pitch classes aside and look at the pattern: there are only two ways to mirror. *Based on a tone:* one note stays fixed and everything pairs around it (Levy's mirror, centered on the generator: C major ↔ F minor). *Based on a pair:* the center sits between two notes, which trade places, and everything pairs around that gap (this book's mirror, with the fifth as the pair: C major ↔ C minor). Coleman's two spirals are these two patterns written out chromatically, each from one starting point: spiral one is tone-based, spiral two is pair-based. A pair-based mirror can be described by any of its pairs: this one swaps C–G, and also E♭–E, A–B♭ and D–F. Coleman names it by its closest pair, the half step; this book names it by the fifth, because the fifth is the unit, the pair that generates the others, including the half step between the two colors. (His sum numbers only make sense counting C as 0; here it is simply the mirror on the fifth.) The Three Diminished Sevenths works through every mirror of both kinds on the lattice.

**What is shared (credit it).**

- **The prime ladder.** Levy's senarius: 2, 3 and 5 for octave, fifth and major third, which Coleman notes alongside Mathieu.
- **Mirrors around the fifth in practice.** Levy's parallel tonic pairs (F major and "−C major," that is, F minor) share the fifth F–C.
- **The down-dominants.** These are Levy's negative dominants. In G, the downward chord G–E♭–C–A substitutes for D7; −F7 sits over C7 as C11♭9. The Ch. 7 and Ch. 15 material stands on this ground.
- **Practitioner support for re-rooting.** Coleman hears Parker's substitutes as "minor iv to I" and "minor ♭vi to I," named from their heard roots.
- **Sum notation.** This book's mirror is Coleman's sum-7 axis; its twins all add to 7 semitones.

**Where this book differs.**

1. **Priority.** Levy treats the generator-centered, symmetric view (absolute conception) as universal, and bottom-up hearing (telluric adaptation) as an earthly habit laid over it. This book reverses that: generation before ancestry makes bottom-up hearing the principle.
2. **No undertones.** Coleman explains the darkness of the minor substitutes by undertone energy. Here darkness is the sign of the 5 coordinate; no undertone series is needed.
3. **The unit.** Levy's generator is one tone; here it is a two-pole fifth, closer to Hauptmann.
4. **The seventh.** Levy makes the dominant's seventh the natural 7:4. This book uses the spine F and stops at 5, which is now a disagreement with a named predecessor (see the boundary at 5 in Open questions).
5. **Coordinates.** Mod-12 sums cannot separate fifths from thirds; lattice addresses can (claims 4 and 5).

**What the coordinates add.** The lattice itself is old (Euler, von Oettingen, Longuet-Higgins, Mathieu), and the flip of a single triad is neo-Riemannian P. What the addresses add:

- **One rule becomes two.** Twins sum to (1, 0): the color numbers are equal and opposite, and the generation numbers total one fifth. A semitone count cannot make that split.
- **Polar, not equal, in the arithmetic.** Home sits at the origin, (0, 0); the mirror's center sits at (½, 0), halfway along the fifth. The gap between them is the asymmetry: color mirrors around the center, the root stays at the origin. Generator-centered dualism has no such gap. *Derived;* the strongest argument for the choice of axis.
- **What equal temperament hides.** A pure third mirrors to a pure third (5/4 ↔ 6/5), and the two Ds, the two B♭s and C♭ vs. B become visible.
