# Notes and Anecdotes

**The Cal Ear Training Class (Anecdote Note):**

- **The Moment:** Sitting in an ear training classroom at UC Berkeley, listening to an interval played down.
- **The Error:** Heard a melodic major third going down, but called it a minor third.
- **The Question:** Struck by a deeper, haunting intuition—*why does a major third stepping downward carry that shadowed, minor-flavored weight?*
- **Why it matters:** Traditional theory treats intervals as flat, linear distance measurements on a keyboard. But the ear hears direction and space—the way downward motion interacts with gravity, ancestry, and the mirror of the fifth. That single classroom moment is the literal seed of the polar framework.


**On Notation and Compression**

The traditional language of music theory—scale names, chord symbols, and equal temperament—isn't a mistake or a rigid cage. It was cooked up purely to communicate these ideas at all; it’s just the language we’ve had.

Scale descriptions are remarkably efficient—almost *too* efficient, to the point of sanding off the depth of the terrain. They compress vast spatial realities into flat shorthand so we can function on the bandstand. But compression always discards data. When we mistake the shortcut for the territory, we lose the mechanical collisions of the lattice, the genetic lineage of our chords, and the forces pulling at the edges.

A holistic framework doesn't throw out the shorthand. It recognizes it for what it is: a brilliant, necessary compression codec. The work isn't to replace the language, but to remember what's hidden underneath it.
