# The argument, restructured

This tab restates the book as a chain of claims, each tagged by what it rests on. The original outline (first tab) is unchanged. Ideas that aren't yet part of the argument are parked at the end, not deleted.

## Thesis

**We hear major and minor as two colors of one home.** Most symmetric theories flip harmony around a single tone, which turns C major into F minor and then has to explain why our ears disagree. This book flips around the fifth instead, so the mirror and the experience coincide: C minor is C major upside down, and nothing needs renaming.

The order of the book follows from this: perception first (one home, two colors), then the unit that preserves it (the fifth), then the coordinates that show the choice is not arbitrary. It is the only flip that keeps the unit whole, and its center sits half a fifth from home, which is why color mirrors while the root stays put.

**Tags.** *Premise*: the book's stance, stated openly, no citation claimed. *Supported*: rests on cited work. *Derived*: follows from earlier claims. *Hypothesis*: stated, to be tested. *Open*: not resolved.

## The founding premise: generation before ancestry

*Premise.* Generation comes first. Pitches are related because one sounds the other as an overtone; that is a fact about the sound. Ancestry is the ear running that relation backward, inferring a source it does not hear. So every offspring claim in the book is about the sound, and every ancestor claim is about listening.

**Supporting ideas**

- **The physics runs one way.** A tone carries its harmonics above it; it does not carry the tones that would have produced it. *Supported:* the harmonic series.
- **Ancestry is inference.** Given harmonics, the ear supplies a fundamental that isn't sounding (the missing fundamental, or residue pitch, after Schouten). Small speakers rely on it: bass enhancement adds the harmonics of a low note the speaker cannot reproduce, and listeners hear the low note anyway. *Supported* for single complex tones. Extending it to chord roots follows Terhardt's virtual-pitch model, developed by Parncutt: *Hypothesis*, adopted.
- **The words follow the premise.** Offspring is what a pitch sounds. Ancestor is what the ear infers sounded it. Any ancestor claim should be restatable as "the ear infers…".

**What the premise settles**

- **The unit forms by generation.** A root and the fifth it generates make a unit. A root whose fifth is absent has no unit: Locrian. A note missing only its ancestor still forms one: Lydian's F lacks B♭ but still generates C.
- **Minor needs no undertone series.** G is a real overtone of C (3rd partial) and of E♭ (5th partial). "Two ancestors, one offspring" is overtones read backward. This keeps the dualist insight (von Oettingen's phonicity) without the dualist physics.
- **The root is the generator.** Gravity is the ear tracing back toward it.

**What it does not settle on its own.** Why the trace stops at home: F generates C, so ancestry alone would keep falling flatward. The working answer is in claim 8: gravity follows the clues that are actually sounding, and nothing sounding supports F.

**Why this is a premise.** No source cited here states the priority of generation over ancestry as a principle of harmony. The book builds on it openly. Hauptmann and Mathieu may come close; check both.

## The chain of claims

1. **Generation before ancestry.** *Premise* (above).
2. **The prime ladder.** 2 gives identity (the octave), 3 generation (fifths), 5 color (thirds). *Supported:* Mathieu, *Harmonic Experience*; Hauptmann is close (octave as unity, fifth as duality, third as their union), and Levy's senarius uses the same three primes.
3. **The unit is the fifth.** The perfect fifth is the first relation that generates a new pitch, so the book treats it as one object with two poles: the lower pole generates, the upper pole is generated. *Premise:* a choice of unit, argued from claims 1 and 2, not proven.
4. **Addresses.** Every pitch has an address, its power of 3 and its power of 5, octaves set aside. C sits at the origin because the anchor is placed there. Addresses name relationships, not tunings. *Derived.*
5. **The mirror.** A half-turn around the center of C–G. Twins' addresses sum to (1, 0): the 5 number flips sign, the 3 numbers balance on one fifth. It leaves two sets in place: C–G (rest) and B–D–F–A♭ (tension). *Derived;* matches negative harmony in practice. An affirmation, not a proof.
6. **Major and minor are the two polar triads.** The unit plus a third hung up from the lower pole, or down from the upper. Both use the same two color intervals (4:5 and 5:6) in opposite order, Zarlino's two divisions of the fifth. Read by generation: major is one generator, two offspring (C sounds E and G); minor is two generators, one shared offspring (C and E♭ both sound G). *Derived* from 1 and 3.
7. **The root follows generation, not the mirror.** Listeners hear C as the root of C minor (*Supported*). The premise says why: the root is the generator of the fifth, and gravity traces back to it. Color mirrors; gravity does not. This answers the dualist root problem without giving up the mirror. *Derived.* The mechanism, the missing fundamental, is established for single tones; its extension to chord roots is a published model (Terhardt, Parncutt): *Hypothesis.*
8. **Home is the best-supported root.** Gravity is the tendency to hear a root when the overtone clues point to one. Each sounding pitch points to roots below it; after the octave, the strongest pointer is the fifth below. Home is the root the sounding pitches support most, and the chain stops where support runs out. *Hypothesis* (open question 1). The slope is a picture of this tendency, not its cause.
9. **Windows and modes.** A window is a run of consecutive fifths. Windows grown up from C and down from G pair as mirrors: the two pentatonics, Lydian and Phrygian. Six of the seven modes pair off; Locrian is the window whose root lacks its fifth (from 1 and 3). *Derived.*
10. **Tension sits at the edges of the keys.** C major (F to B) and C minor (A♭ to D) have the tritones F–B and A♭–D at their edges; together they make B°7, the mirror's fixed tension. Each resolves crossed, one voice to a pole and one to the color: B–F to C–E, A♭–D to G–E♭. *Derived.*

## Terms to settle before writing further

- **Color vs. brightness.** Proposal: *color* is the sign of the 5 number (thirds, Y). *Brightness* is a window's lean along the spine (fifths, X), as in the brightness ladder. Related, not the same: Lydian is the brightest window but has no color in the 5 sense.
- **Anchorless.** Proposal: a mode or chord whose bottom note lacks its fifth above. Two kinds: *rootless* (augmented, diminished: no fifth anywhere) and *re-rooted* (m7♭5: a fifth elsewhere that the ear may take as root).
- **Down-dominant.** Proposal: named from its top note, as in the jazz chapter. The original Ch. 7 chord (C A♭ F D) is then the down-dominant from C, heard as Fm6.
- **Counting windows.** By notes: "seven-note window," not "seven fifths."
- **Order.** Key terms go before their first use.

## Two axes, one mirror: beside Levy and Coleman

Source: Steve Coleman's essay "Symmetrical Movement Concept," including his summary of Ernst Levy's *A Theory of Harmony*. This is Coleman's reading of Levy; verify in Levy before citing.

**Two axes.** Levy flips around a single tone, the generator: C–E–G read downward from C is C–A♭–F, F minor. The intervals match, but the heard root moves to F, so the root has to be explained afterward (telluric adaptation). This book flips around the middle of the fifth C–G, Coleman's "sum 7" axis (between E♭ and E, and between A and B♭): C–E–G becomes G–E♭–C, C minor. The fifth stays put; only the third flips. The two axes sit half a fifth apart, so their results land a fifth apart. Every flip exists in both systems; what differs is which one the theory is built on.

**Why Levy centers on a tone.** Not an oversight: it follows from his starting point. If overtones and undertones are equally real, the series is symmetric around one tone, so the center has to be a tone, the generator, with its poles the fifths on either side (F–C–G). Generation before ancestry removes that commitment. Once undertones are inference, nothing pins the center to a tone, and the first generated relation, the fifth, becomes the unit. And since telluric adaptation already explains which root we hear, the generator at the center of absolute conception does no perceptual work. This book keeps the part of Levy that matches hearing, his telluric gravity, and moves the center into the fifth. Levy described the poles; his undertone commitment put them around a tone instead of inside a fifth.

**The unit makes its own axis.** In Coleman's spirals every axis is a unison or a half step, and the middle of C–G is the half-step axis E♭–E (sum 7; spiral out and you reach D–F, D♭–F♯, then C–G). Coleman chooses an axis and spirals outward; he does not single out this one or tie it to the fifth. Here it falls out of the unit: E is generated (the 5th partial of C, offspring of the lower pole), and E♭ is inferred (G is its 5th partial, so it is the source the ear supplies under the upper pole). One color sounded up from the bottom, one implied down from the top: the premise, generation before ancestry, shows up inside the symmetry, and it is why minor fuses less. The coordinates agree: E = (0, 1) and E♭ = (1, −1) have their midpoint at (½, 0), the mirror's center. The step between them is the chromatic semitone (25/24), the P move, which equal temperament flattens into Coleman's plain half step. So the half-step axis is not an assumption here; it is the two colors the fifth creates. *Derived.* Possibly a section of its own.

**Note: two kinds of mirror, and Coleman's spirals.** Set pitch classes aside and look at the pattern: there are only two ways to mirror. *Based on a tone:* one note stays fixed and everything pairs around it (Levy's mirror, centered on the generator: C major ↔ F minor). *Based on a pair:* the center sits between two notes, which trade places, and everything pairs around that gap (this book's mirror, with the fifth as the pair: C major ↔ C minor). Coleman's two spirals are these two patterns written out chromatically, each from one starting point: spiral one is tone-based, spiral two is pair-based. A pair-based mirror can be described by any of its pairs: this one swaps C–G, and also E♭–E, A–B♭ and D–F. Coleman names it by its closest pair, the half step; this book names it by the fifth, because the fifth is the unit, the pair that generates the others, including the half step between the two colors. (His sum numbers only make sense counting C as 0; here it is simply the mirror on the fifth.)

**What is shared (credit it).**

- **The prime ladder.** Levy's senarius: 2, 3 and 5 for octave, fifth and major third, which Coleman notes alongside Mathieu.
- **Mirrors around the fifth in practice.** Levy's parallel tonic pairs (F major and "−C major," that is, F minor) share the fifth F–C.
- **The down-dominants.** These are Levy's negative dominants. In G, the downward chord G–E♭–C–A substitutes for D7; −F7 sits over C7 as C11♭9. The Ch. 7 and Ch. 15 material stands on this ground.
- **Practitioner support for re-rooting.** Coleman hears Parker's substitutes as "minor iv to I" and "minor ♭vi to I," named from their heard roots.
- **Sum notation.** This book's mirror is Coleman's sum-7 axis; its twins all add to 7 semitones.

**Where this book differs.**

1. **Priority.** Levy treats the generator-centered, symmetric view (absolute conception) as universal, and bottom-up hearing (telluric adaptation) as an earthly habit laid over it. This book reverses that: generation before ancestry makes bottom-up hearing the principle.
2. **No undertones.** Coleman explains the darkness of the minor substitutes by undertone energy. Here darkness is the sign of the 5 coordinate; no undertone series is needed.
3. **The unit.** Levy's generator is one tone; here it is a two-pole fifth, closer to Hauptmann.
4. **The seventh.** Levy makes the dominant's seventh the natural 7:4. This book uses the spine F and stops at 5, which is now a disagreement with a named predecessor (open question 3).
5. **Coordinates.** Mod-12 sums cannot separate fifths from thirds; lattice addresses can (claims 4 and 5).

**What the coordinates add.** The lattice itself is old (Euler, von Oettingen, Longuet-Higgins, Mathieu), and the flip of a single triad is neo-Riemannian P. What the addresses add:

- **One rule becomes two.** Twins sum to (1, 0): the color numbers are equal and opposite, and the generation numbers total one fifth. A semitone count cannot make that split.
- **Polar, not equal, in the arithmetic.** Home sits at the origin, (0, 0); the mirror's center sits at (½, 0), halfway along the fifth. The gap between them is the asymmetry: color mirrors around the center, the root stays at the origin. Generator-centered dualism has no such gap. *Derived;* the strongest argument for the choice of axis.
- **What equal temperament hides.** A pure third mirrors to a pure third (5/4 ↔ 6/5), and the two Ds, the two B♭s and C♭ vs. B become visible.

**The contribution, stated plainly.** Not the lattice, not the flip, not upside-down symmetry. The book combines the global flip, lattice coordinates and a root fixed at the bottom of the fifth, starting from how major and minor are heard. Its claim is clarity: matching the symmetric world to the heard one without translation. That claim is itself to be tested on readers.

In C–G, G points to C; C points to F, but nothing sounding supports F, so C wins. This matches virtual-pitch models of chord roots, where (in Parncutt's version) a pitch's strongest support after the octave goes to the pitch a fifth below, then a major third below; check the weights before citing. 2. **The concordance.** Reread Levy (telluric adaptation may equal gravity here), Hauptmann (fifth as duality) and von Oettingen (phonicity). Each may already own one of claims 3, 6 and 7. Working hypothesis from Coleman's summary: telluric adaptation is this book's gravity premise with the priority reversed (see Two axes, one mirror). 3. **The boundary at 5.** B♭ is C's 7th partial: offspring, by the premise. State that the book stops at 5 by choice, and whether 7 changes anything. ### Working note: the express lanes

*Exploration, not yet a claim.* It started from the sound of even and odd intervals. The intervals that split into equal even halves (major third into whole steps, minor sixth into major thirds) build the whole-tone scale and the augmented triad: no fifth anywhere, nothing to orient them. Diminished floats for a different reason: its minor thirds each carry a fifth's worth of generation, but four of them close a cycle and cancel. That pointed back to the two third-lanes.

**What each step is made of.**

- **Fifth (1, 0):** pure generation. The local road, where gravity works.
- **Major third (0, 1):** pure color, no generation.
- **Minor third (1, −1):** one fifth of generation plus one step of dark color.

**Why the major-third lane leaves.** In equal temperament a major third up counts as four fifths (E major has four sharps). In the lattice it is one color step: the same E reached two ways, a comma apart (Ch. 4, The Second Path). So the move is harmonically one step but a long jump along the spine. That is the chromatic-mediant feeling, close through a common tone and far by key signature, with nothing generational carrying home along.

**Why the minor-third lane stays in the family.** Alternating P (parallel) and R (relative) walks the minor-third cycle: C, C minor, E♭, E♭ minor, G♭. Alternating P and L (leading-tone exchange) walks the major-third cycle: C, C minor, A♭, A♭ minor, E. *Supported:* standard neo-Riemannian results, developed by Cohn (octatonic and hexatonic cycles). Read in this book's terms:

- **P** flips home's color and keeps the unit, so the anchor stays.
- **R** moves to the relative and keeps the collection.
- **L** keeps neither the anchor nor the collection.

The minor-third lane is built entirely from moves that each keep something of home; the major-third lane needs a move that keeps nothing. Its first stops are the relatives of home's two colors: A minor for the bright side, E♭ major for the dark. One hop on the minor-third lane never leaves home's family; one hop on the major-third lane already has. *Derived,* using cited tools. This replaces the outline's "three fifths vs. four" as the reason.

**To check:** whether the P, R and L reading holds for minor-key homes, and how Giant Steps and the diminished pivot read through it.

### Working note: the unit, the poles and the three diminished sevenths

*Exploration, not yet a claim.* Two kinds of source generate pitch sets by mirroring. The polar unit is a pair, so its mirror is pair-based. A single pitch (a pole, or one of the unit's color tones) gives a tone-based mirror. This note works in lattice addresses throughout; twelve-tone sums appear in parentheses as readouts.

**Notation: the lattice sum.** For any mirror, add the addresses of any pair of twins: the result is always the same vector, S = (s3, s5), twice the mirror's center. This is the book's rule that twins add up to (1, 0), applied to every mirror. The twelve-tone sum used in negative harmony and in Coleman's spirals is a readout of S: 7·s3 + 4·s5 (mod 12), since a fifth is 7 semitones and a major third 4. Many lattice sums read out to the same number, differing by commas and dieses, which is what equal temperament erases. The twelve-tone sum names a key-level mirror; S names its address.

- **s3, the generation sum.** Its parity is the parity of the twelve-tone sum, so the odd/even distinction survives the lift exactly. Odd: the center sits halfway along a fifth (pair-based). Even: it sits on a tone (tone-based).
- **s5, the color sum.** Zero: the center is on the spine, and the mirror flips bright and dark around it. Nonzero: the center sits on a color layer.

**Two lines of centers cross at the unit.** Along the spine, centers step by half a fifth: poles on tones, the unit and the anchors on the midpoints between them (each step adds 7 to the twelve-tone sum). Through the same center runs a second line: from the unit, S changes by (−1, 2) to reach E and by (1, −2) to reach E♭. That vector is the chromatic semitone, E♭ to E, the P move. So the color-tone spirals (8 and 6) are not an equal-temperament artifact: they run along the color-flip direction, while the poles and anchors run along the generation direction. In twelve-tone notation both look like neighboring sums; the lattice shows they are different directions.

**From the unit: (1, 0), sum 7.** The spiral starts at the unit's own chromatic pair, E♭–E, and runs out through D–F, D♭–F♯, C–G and B–A♭ to the second half-step pair, A–B♭. Crossing the two half-step pairs gives the border tritones E–B♭ and E♭–A.

**From the color tones: (0, 2) and (2, −2), sums 8 and 6.** Taken one at a time, each color tone starts a tone-based spiral.

- **From E (sum 8):** E–E, E♭–F, D–F♯, D♭–G, C–G♯, B–A, B♭–B♭. In equal temperament E–B♭ is held in place and D♭–G swapped.
- **From E♭ (sum 6):** E♭–E♭, D–E, D♭–F, C–F♯, B–G, B♭–A♭, A–A. E♭–A is held; C–F♯ swapped.

The border tritones of the unit's spiral are the axes of these two: the unit's mirror exchanges the two color-tone mirrors.

**From the poles: (0, 0) and (2, 0), the minor-third spines.** Each pole runs its minor-third spine both ways. Down a minor third adds (−1, 1), bright; up adds (1, −1), dark.

- **C:** C A F♯ (bright) and C E♭ G♭ (dark).
- **G:** G E C♯ (bright) and G B♭ D♭ (dark).
- **F, the ancestor, (−2, 0):** F D B (bright) and F A♭ C♭ (dark).

Each spine is symmetric under the half-turn around its pole. The first ring around each pole is a border tritone: A and E♭ around C, E and B♭ around G.

**Diminished sevenths are minor-third lines.** A minor third, (1, −1), leaves a + b unchanged, so the lattice's minor-third lines are the lines where a + b is constant. A diminished seventh in equal temperament is the family of these lines with the same a + b (mod 3), merged. A mirror maps a + b to (s3 + s5) − (a + b), so it holds a line exactly when s3 + s5 is even, and trades lines in pairs when it is odd.

So the color tone and the pole agree exactly in the lattice: each pair is one half-turn family on one minor-third line, centered at different notes of it. E sits one minor third below G on G's line; E♭ one minor third above C on C's line. Note the crossing: the bright tone goes with the upper pole, the dark tone with the lower. And the twelve-tone statement "sum 7 holds B°7" lifts to something sharper: the unit's mirror trades two parallel minor-third segments, the bright B–D and the dark F–A♭. That is the hexagon's B°7 and its bright and dark tritones. Coverage of all twelve notes by three diminished sevenths is arithmetic; the line structure is what the lattice adds.

**Three species of tritone.** Add a tritone's two hexagon addresses and read the color number. F–B and C–F♯ are bright; A♭–D and D♭–G are dark. E–B♭ gives (2, 0) and E♭–A gives (0, 0), so their color cancels: each joins one bright tone to one dark one. The neutral pair splits by pole instead: E–B♭ centers on G, E♭–A on C. They are the first rings of the pole spines, which is why they cancel. *Derived;* species depend on address (see What the lattice decides).

**Edges.** Slide a seven-note window past C–G. Each slide moves the edge tritone by a fifth, and 7 ≡ 1 (mod 3), so the edges cycle through the three diminished sevenths with period three.

- **The neutral tritones are the edges of the twin palindromes.** The centered six-note window B♭ F C G D A has B♭–A at its edges; one more note either way gives E–B♭ or E♭–A.
- **Each diminished seventh is the joint edge set of two windows three fifths apart,** one hop on the minor-third lane: Ionian and Aeolian, Mixolydian and Phrygian, Lydian and Dorian.
- **Edge color runs bright, bright, neutral, neutral, dark, dark:** placement sets color at the edges too (principle 6).
- **Six windows, three mirror pairs.** Lydian and Phrygian, Ionian and Aeolian, Mixolydian and Dorian: each tritone appears once, and each diminished seventh twice. Locrian adds no new edge. Its G♭–C is Lydian's C–F♯ reached from the other end of the spine (see The tritone as antipode).

**Resolution.** In the diatonic setting E–B♭ resolves inward to F–A (IV, bright) and E♭–A to D–B♭ (the top of v, dark); the unit's mirror swaps the two, as it swaps IV and v. B°7 resolves into the home anchor; the border tritones resolve into the flanking anchors, crossed: the bright tone's tension falls flatward to F, the dark tone's rises toward G. Each pole's spine is also the rootless 7♭9 into its offspring, with the pole as seventh (F's into C, C's into G, G's into D): the ancestral F in G7 (Ch. 7), generalized. *Hypothesis.*

**Blue notes on the axis.** The unit's axis crosses the chromatic circle between E♭ and E and between A and B♭. In continuous pitch its fixed points are 350 and 950 cents: the neutral third, and the zone between 6 and ♭7. The border tritones are those of I7 (C7: E–B♭) and IV7 (F7: A–E♭) in a blues, and the mirror swaps them. The harmonic seventh (7:4, about 969 cents) sits near the second fixed point, which bears on open question 3. *Hypothesis, to test.*

**What the lattice decides.**

- **Direction, not exactness.** Every mirror here is a true half-turn of the lattice. The spine-centered ones (poles, unit, anchors) flip color around the spine. The color-tone ones are centered on a color layer, so they send the spine to the doubly bright layer (E) or the doubly dark layer (E♭); they keep their own minor-third line instead.
- **The arms don't meet.** Two minor thirds down from C reach F♯ at 25/18 (569 cents); two up reach G♭ at 36/25 (631 cents), a greater diesis apart. The equal-tempered tritone, 600 cents, is their exact midpoint: the neutral tritone. Spelling records which arm a note came from.
- **Two B°7s.** F's spine gives F, D at 10/9, B at 50/27, A♭ at 8/5 and C♭ at 48/25; its D is ii's D, so it reads as predominant. The hexagon's B°7 is F, D at 9/8, B at 15/8 and A♭: V's D and the true leading tone, the dominant reading. They share only F and A♭, and A♭–D is dark in one reading and neutral in the other. Same keys, a comma apart: the two Ds (Regions) again.
- **Neutral from home, colored from a neighbor.** E–B♭ and E♭–A are neutral when read from C–G. Read from the anchors beside it they take color (next working note).

**The tritone as antipode.** *Exploration.* On a circle, every mirror has two centers half a circle apart. The unit's axis crosses the circle of fifths at C–G and again at F♯–C♯ (G♭–D♭); on the chromatic circle it crosses between E♭ and E and between A and B♭, the two blue-note points, a tritone apart. A pole's mirror holds the pole and its tritone (C and F♯). So in equal temperament everything mirrored at home is mirrored again at the tritone, and tritone transposition leaves every diminished seventh in place. B°7 is the rootless 7♭9 of both G and D♭ (the tritone substitution), so home's tension resolves to C or to its antipode, F♯, as well as along the minor-third lane.

In the lattice the spine is a line, so there is no antipode: the far point splits in two. Six fifths up from C reaches F♯, the bright extreme; six down reaches G♭, the dark extreme, a Pythagorean comma apart. Two minor thirds down and up reach F♯ and G♭ a greater diesis apart. Every such pair is centered on C and swapped exactly by C's own mirror; equal temperament merges them, so the pole's mirror appears to hold its tritone. The tritone is where the open spines meet themselves halfway around.

So there are two polarities. The unit's is local: generation and color around a fifth. The tritone's is global: the circle's antipode, real in equal temperament, and in the lattice the place the comma lands, where bright and dark extremes are merged by closure. Lydian's C–F♯ and Locrian's G♭–C are the same edge reached from opposite ends of the spine. Ties to Ch. 10 (the circle that can't close) and Ch. 11 (F♯ and G♭).

### Working note: inside and outside diminished, from the anchors

*Exploration, a candidate derivation.* Each anchor of the window F–C, C–G, G–D is a polar unit with its own mirror: (−1, 0), (1, 0) and (3, 0), sums 5, 7 and 9. All three are pair-based and centered on the spine, the same kind as home's mirror, so each carries its own key's tension exactly as C–G carries B°7. Home's diminished seventh is the inside one; the subdominant and dominant anchors give the two outside ones.

| Side | Anchor | Lattice sum (ET) | Bright segment | Dark segment | Diminished 7th | Resolves into |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Subdominant | F–C | (−1, 0) (5) | E–G | B♭–D♭ | C♯°7 | F |
| Home | C–G | (1, 0) (7) | B–D | F–A♭ | B°7 | C |
| Dominant | G–D | (3, 0) (9) | F♯–A | C–E♭ | C°7 | G |

- **Outside means reach.** A diminished seventh resolves four ways, into the notes a half step above its members, each a hop apart on the minor-third lane. Home's B°7 reaches C, E♭, G♭ and A: home's own minor-third family, the shared territory of parallel and relative keys, which is why it feels like it closes. The outside sets reach the other two families: C°7 into G, B♭, D♭ and E (vii°7 of V), C♯°7 into F, A♭, B and D (vii°7 of IV). Together they reach every root home's tension does not, so the cadential energy of the anchors beside home tends to carry the ear further afield.
- **Same structure, transposed by the spine.** Each anchor's mirror holds no minor-third line; it trades two parallel segments, one bright and one dark, as home's mirror trades B–D and F–A♭. The tritones are the edges of that anchor's major and minor collections: F–C has E–B♭ (F major) and D♭–G (F minor); G–D has C–F♯ (G major) and E♭–A (G minor).
- **Crossed resolution on every anchor.** Subdominant side: E–B♭ to F–A, D♭–G to C–A♭. Dominant side: F♯–C to G–B, E♭–A to D–B♭. In each, one voice lands on a pole and one on the color, as B–F to C–E and A♭–D to G–E♭ at home.
- **Two routes to each tension.** The tension into a pole is held by that pole's own unit mirror and by its ancestor's minor-third spine. Into C: the C–G mirror and F's spine (B°7). Into G: the G–D mirror and C's spine (C°7). Into F: the F–C mirror and B♭'s spine (C♯°7).
- **Two routes to the outside sets.** The anchors reach the outside diminished sevenths along the spine; the color tones reach the same twelve-tone sets along the chromatic semitone, crossed: the bright tone E with the subdominant side, the dark tone E♭ with the dominant side.
- **The neutral tritones take color from a neighbor.** E–B♭ is neutral from home (E is home's bright third, B♭ the dark floor under G–D), but bright from F–C, where E is F's leading tone and B♭ F's ancestor on the spine. E♭–A is neutral from home but dark from G–D, where E♭ is G's dark color and A G's offspring on the spine. The same keys change address with the anchor heard as home, so these tritones are where home's reading and its neighbors' readings disagree.

This gives the anchor window IV I V a tension for each anchor: three units, three diminished sevenths, inside and outside. It fits the book's dominant and subdominant sides directly and deserves its own section once tested. *Derived* for the structure; *Hypothesis* for the reading as dominant- and subdominant-side energies.

**Working rule.** Twelve-tone and chord-scale names identify keys; lattice addresses identify relationships. Every mod-12 statement is restated as a lattice statement plus the comma or diesis equal temperament erased. A diminished seventh is a key-level object that the lattice splits into versions (predominant and dominant, bright arm and dark arm, inside and outside), and the ear chooses by context.

**To check:** whether the ear reads a given diminished chord as predominant or dominant as the addresses predict; whether the neutral tritones (E–B♭, E♭–A) sound less directed than the bright and dark ones, and gain direction when a flanking anchor is heard as home; the blue-note axis against measured blues intonation; how the outside diminished sevenths read in minor-key homes.

### Working note: the gentle slope

**The slope is a visual cue, not a coordinate.** Tilt the whole XY floor slightly so generated fifths sit higher. It is a reminder of what pitch does: stacked fifths climb, as the overtone series does. The slope belongs to the world, not to the notes. The mirror moves notes across the floor; down stays put, so gravity does not mirror. The anchor only sets sea level. Computation does not use it.

### Working note: the septimal mesh (Z as the 7th partial)

*Exploration, not yet a claim.* Earmarked for a Beyond 5 chapter. An enhanced view of the polar mirror: the 5-limit floor with the 7th partial as a third dimension. Use it only if it tells a story when visualized; the energy discussion is already supported by the 5-limit.

**Z is the power of 7.** Every pitch gets a third address number, (a, b, c). Each node has two neighbors in Z:

- **Up:** its own 7th partial, which it sounds (offspring). C up is B♭ at 7/4.
- **Down:** the note whose 7th partial it is, which the ear infers (ancestor). C down is D at 8/7.

So the mesh is three decks, each a full copy of the floor: Z = 0 (the 5-limit floor), Z = +1 (every node's 7th partial) and Z = −1 (every node's septimal ancestor). Up still means generated, the same sense as the slope.

**The mirror in the mesh.** (a, b, c) → (1 − a, −b, −c). Twins sum to (1, 0, 0), and the decks swap crosswise, as E and E♭ do:

| Sounded (7 up) | Mirror twin (7 down) |
| --- | --- |
| C's 7th partial: B♭ 7/4 (0, 0, 1) | G's septimal ancestor: A 12/7 (1, 0, −1) |
| G's 7th partial: F 21/16 (1, 0, 1) | C's septimal ancestor: D 8/7 (0, 0, −1) |
| F's 7th partial: E♭ 7/6 (−1, 0, 1) | D's septimal ancestor: E 9/7 (2, 0, −1) |

The mirror swaps sounded and inferred along both 5 (Y) and 7 (Z). Only the 3 direction (X) keeps its root, because gravity runs along generation by fifths. Color mirrors in two dimensions; gravity still does not.

**7 intensifies color, with the lineage crossed.** Each septimal note sits one septimal diesis (36/35, about 49 cents) further from neutral than its 5-limit neighbor:

| 5-limit | Septimal neighbor | Side |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6/5 (316¢) | 7/6 (267¢) | darker |
| 5/4 (386¢) | 9/7 (435¢) | brighter |
| 9/5 (1018¢) | 7/4 (969¢) | darker |
| 5/3 (884¢) | 12/7 (933¢) | brighter |

Twins straddle the mirror's fixed points, 351¢ and 951¢: the blue-note axis. With 5 the sounded note is bright (E); with 7 the sounded notes are dark (7/4, 7/6) and the inferred ones bright. Offspring is bright only for the 5th partial, not for generation in general. That may be the cleanest argument for stopping the color dimension at 5.

**What it says about function.**

- **The mirror of C7 is Cm6.** C E G B♭(7/4) mirrors to C E♭ G A(12/7): the down-dominant's m6 reading, with a reason in the tuning.
- **A third D for the down-dominant.** G B D F(21/16) mirrors to C A♭ F D(8/7, 231¢), beside the 9/8 and 10/9 candidates (Ch. 7).
- **What equal temperament erases most.** The harmonic seventh is about 31 cents off the tempered B♭, the septimal thirds about 33 off. The decks make that visible: readouts, not sources.

**Two views.** The mesh shows how a note is generated. Flattened by pitch, each septimal node settles beside its 5-limit neighbor as a satellite, which shows where it sounds. The pairing matches the lattice and its scale readouts.

**Drawing.** Keep the slope gentle so the decks read as vertical. Draw decks ±1 as faint ghosts and light only the nodes in use; 7² relations rarely matter. Introduce the decks at the dominant seventh, the blues and the boundary at 5.

*Derived* for the addresses and the mirror; *Hypothesis* for the functional readings.

**To check:** whether listeners hear 7/4 as darker than 9/5 with context controlled; how measured blues thirds (often near neutral) sit against 7/6 and 9/7; whether the crossed lineage changes the arrival order of the dark degrees.

### Working note: the 12-bar blues

*A reading for Ch. 17, not yet a claim.* The blues lives on the anchor window IV I V and draws on both its sides. It mixes energies: the subdominant cadence is essential beside the dominant one, and neutral tritones give its sevenths their color. It is a vast family of forms; this reads one common skeleton and how variations sit on it. Everything here is 5-limit.

The blues is an idiom with its own logic, rooted in African American practice. This reading shows where its harmony sits on the lattice, not where it came from. Blue notes are inflections, not tunings.

**The form on the anchors.** I, IV and V stand on F–C, C–G and G–D: the centered anchor window, its own mirror (see Anchor windows). The skeleton below circles inside that window.

| Bars | Chord (in C) | Tritone | From home | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1–4 | C7 | E–B♭ | neutral | Color at home; turns toward F in bar 4 |
| 5–6 | F7 | A–E♭ | neutral | Subdominant side; E♭ is home's ♭3 |
| 7–8 | C7 | E–B♭ | neutral | Subdominant cadence: IV to I, E♭ to E |
| 9 | G7 | B–F | bright | Dominant side: half of B°7 |
| 10 | F7 | A–E♭ | neutral | V to IV: tension dissolves rather than resolves |
| 11–12 | C7, G7 | E–B♭, B–F | neutral, bright | Subdominant cadence home, then the authentic turnaround |

**Why a dominant seventh can be home.** Read from C–G, E–B♭ is neutral: E is home's bright third, B♭ the dark floor under G–D, and their colors cancel. Nothing points urgently, so C7 can sit as tonic. The ♭7 is a favored color, not a demand.

**The motion baked into ♭7 (bar 4).** Read from F–C, the same tritone turns bright and directed: E becomes F's leading tone, B♭ F's ancestor on the spine. It resolves inward, E to F and B♭ to A. When bar 4 accents the ♭7, often the first place a version adds it, the ear starts hearing F–C as the next anchor. Same keys, new address: re-addressing by context, with no change of tuning.

**Two cadences, both sides of the window.** The blues needs both. The authentic cadence (V to I) is gravity: the root falls a fifth to its generator. The subdominant cadence (IV to I) runs the other way: the root rises a fifth, ancestor to offspring, as F generates C. The blues comes home this way at bars 6–7 and 10–11, and IV7 to I also flips E♭ to E, dark to bright. Bar 4 adds a third motion: I7 acting as the dominant of IV, gravity pointing flatward. The form mixes gravity, generation and color rather than resting on one. *Hypothesis:* the subdominant pull is generation heard forward, the authentic pull generation traced back (Ch. 13).

**The sevenths on I.** Three choices sit on the I chord, and they lean different ways:

- **B♭ (C7):** dark, subdominant side. Leans outward, toward A and F, the neighboring anchor. The favored blues color, because the form spends its energy between I and IV.
- **B (Cmaj7):** bright, dominant side. Leans inward, up into C. Parker's "Blues for Alice" opens this way (Fmaj7 in F).
- **A (C6):** B♭'s mirror twin. Common on I in swing-era blues.

Polar, not equal: both sevenths are colors on I, but B leans into home and B♭ leans away from it.

**The blue notes sit on the axis.** The blues' signature inflections are the unit's two half-step pairs: E♭–E (♭3 against 3) and A–B♭ (6 against ♭7). The mirror's axis runs between each pair, with fixed points at 351¢ and 951¢. The IV7 to I move carries E♭ to E, the chromatic semitone, the color flip (P). The blue fifth, ♭5 at 600¢, is the midpoint of the two arms that don't meet (C's F♯ and G♭; see The tritone as antipode). *Hypothesis:* the three blue notes are the three places the mirror lands between keys. Test against measured blues intonation.

**Only the light flats.** The blues adds ♭3 and ♭7 to a major tonic and leaves out ♭6 and ♭2. That matches the arrival order: ♭3 and ♭7 color dyads at or above home, while ♭6 and ♭2 color dyads below it and are never heard as bright (see Arrival).

**V to IV (bars 9–10).** Classical harmony rarely moves V to IV; the blues does every chorus. Here the bright, directed B–F gives way to the neutral A–E♭. The tension is not resolved but dissolved, and the cadence waits for bar 11. *Hypothesis.*

**The mirrored blues.** C7 mirrors to Cm6 (C E♭ G A), F7 to Gm6 (G B♭ D E) and G7 to Fm6 (F A♭ C D). The mirror of a major blues is a minor blues with the roles swapped: IV's place becomes v, V's becomes iv. A candidate for the Gallery of Mirrors (Ch. 16).

**Variations.** The blues is a vast family: 8-, 12- and 16-bar forms, minor blues, jazz and bebop blues, countless turnarounds. The skeleton above is one member. Variations change which colors and anchors light up; a few, read the same way:

- **Quick change (IV in bar 2):** an early visit to the subdominant side.
- **ii–V into IV (bar 4, Gm7–C7):** makes the re-addressing toward F explicit.
- **♯iv°7 in bar 6 (F♯°7 to C/G):** C°7, the dominant side's outside diminished seventh, resolving into G in the bass (see the inside and outside working note).
- **ii–V in bars 9–10 (Dm7–G7):** replaces V–IV with an authentic approach.
- **Bird blues:** descending ii–Vs reach outside the window by the express lanes; a reading to work out.

Each is a readout of the same anchors; the lattice tracks what changes.

*Derived* for the tritone species, the anchor window and the mirrored chords; *Hypothesis* for the blue-note axis and the V–IV reading.

**To check:** where bar 4's ♭7 enters across recorded versions; whether listeners hear C7 on I as less directed than G7 on V, and F7 as more directed once F–C is heard as home; measured blues thirds, fifths and sevenths against 351, 600 and 951 cents; minor blues read the same way.
